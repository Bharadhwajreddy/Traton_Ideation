The problem, in plain words

An electric truck has a huge battery. Most of the day it sits in the depot doing nothing. V2G ("vehicle-to-grid") means using that parked battery to store cheap energy and sell it back expensive, or to support the power grid — earning money while the truck waits.

The catch: every time you charge and discharge, you wear the battery a little. So the real question is not "can a truck do V2G" — it can. The question is:

How do we squeeze the most money out of the parked battery while protecting its life, its warranty, and the truck's ability to do its actual job — across different countries, batteries and missions?

A good thesis answers a sharp slice of that question with a new method, and proves it with real data. The rest of this document is about finding that slice.

The diesel-era lens (your instinct — and it's a good one)

The combustion era made its biggest leaps by turning experience into reusable maps and records that everyone downstream could trust. Three of those map directly onto batteries — and they quietly motivate the strongest ideas in this document:

Diesel / petrol eraBattery-era equivalentWhat it unlocks
Verbrauchskennfeld — the engine fuel/efficiency map built from dyno tests V2G-degradation map — how fast the pack wears vs. power, charge level, temperature The charger can predict wear at any operating point and back off automatically
Scheckheft — the stamped service logbook that travels with the car Battery passport — the lifecycle record that travels with the pack Any charger the truck visits knows the battery's history
Knock control — retard ignition just before the engine knocks Plating-limit control — back off charge rate just before lithium plating Maximum performance without damaging the battery, adjusted as it ages
Diesel-era analogy Nobody could predict a diesel engine's fuel use until someone ran structured dyno tests and fit a surface to the data. Your real-truck V2G test (idea A2) does exactly that for battery wear — it builds the battery's "fuel map" (idea A3).

⭐ My recommendation: one unified thesis that ties everything together

Your latest ideas (battery passport + the degradation test) are not separate from the earlier ones — they are the missing glue. Put together, they form a single, coherent, genuinely novel thesis that only an OEM with a real truck can write:

"The Battery-Passport-Informed Charging Controller" — a depot optimizer that, the moment a truck plugs in, reads the battery's life history, looks up a measured wear map, prices the warranty it would consume, decides the safest profitable charge/V2G plan, and writes the session back to the passport — validated on a real MAN truck and generalised into a decision matrix across countries, chemistries and missions.

In plain words The battery carries a "logbook" (passport). When the truck plugs in, the controller reads the logbook, checks a "wear map" to see how risky each option is, checks how much warranty each option would burn, then picks the best plan. After charging, it stamps the logbook with what happened — a closed loop. Everything else in this document is one block of this picture.
Why it matters The digital twin / simulation is just the laboratory. The contribution is this closed-loop function, which nobody has built for heavy trucks. It is timely (passport mandatory 2027, German V2G rules fixed for 2026, megawatt V2G now demonstrated), and it ends in a product MAN can actually sell.

How the pieces snap together

  • Passport (A1) = the data backbone. Its "state of health" number is the warranty budget.
  • Real-truck test (A2) → wear map (A3) = the physics, and your unfair advantage.
  • Warranty shadow price (B1) = the economics that decides go / no-go.
  • Age-adaptive control (B2) on a day-ahead→intra-day optimizer = the control brain.
  • Per-truck value (B3) + decision matrix (D2) + EU/China compiler (D1) = the outputs people use.

If you want lower risk, lead with the optimizer and treat the passport+warranty loop as the novel chapter. If you want maximum novelty, lead with the closed-loop function. Same building blocks either way — see the questions that decide which.

Foundation — the three ideas built on your advantages

A1 · Battery passport as a live control input (not just paperwork)

NEW — your ideaNovelty: HighTRATON value: High
Make the battery's lifecycle record something the charger reads and writes in real time to make decisions, instead of a document filed away for compliance.
flowchart TB
    PP["Battery Passport (cloud): cycle history, age, temperature history, state of health"] --> PLUG["Truck plugs in"]
    PLUG --> READ["Optimizer READS the passport"]
    READ --> DEC["Decide: max charge rate, charge-level window, V2G yes/no"]
    DEC --> RUN["Run the charge / V2G session"]
    RUN --> LOG["Measure throughput, rate, temperature, health"]
    LOG --> WRITE["WRITE the session back to the passport"]
    WRITE --> PP
In plain words Today the EU is forcing every big battery to carry a digital "passport" of its history from 2027. But that passport is just a record that gets updated rarely. The idea: plug it into the charger's brain so that an older or hotter battery automatically gets gentler treatment, and every session updates the record. A closed loop.
Diesel-era analogy The stamped service logbook (Scheckheft) — except here the mechanic is the charger, and it reads and stamps the book automatically, every time.
Why it matters This is the single idea that unifies the whole thesis: the passport's health value is the warranty budget (B1), its history feeds the age-adaptive rules (B2), and the written-back sessions are the data that build the wear map (A3).
Watch out The official passport updates only on "material change" (resale, repair) — it is not real-time. So for live control you lean on the truck's own BMS health estimate, and use the passport as the cross-charger, cross-owner record. Realistic thesis scope = one depot's closed loop; "every charger everywhere" is the future-work vision.

A2 · Your controlled degradation test (validated — and corrected to be rigorous)

NEW — your ideaYour instinct is rightNeeds the real truck
Drive a fixed bit of range, do a measured V2G session, drive the same bit again, and watch how the battery's behaviour shifts — to learn how V2G wears the pack.
flowchart TB
    R1["Reference test BEFORE: controlled partial discharge in a fixed charge window"] --> M1["Measure capacity, internal resistance, voltage curve"]
    M1 --> V2G["4-hour V2G block at a known rate, charge window and temperature"]
    V2G --> REST["REST: let temperature and chemistry settle"]
    REST --> R2["Reference test AFTER: repeat exactly"]
    R2 --> M2["Measure again -> change in resistance and capacity"]
    M2 --> FIT["Feed differences into a wear model and extrapolate to years"]
In plain words Your original plan — drive 60%→50%, do V2G, drive again, compare the kilometres — is the right idea. But kilometres are a blunt ruler. We swap the ruler for sensitive electrical measurements (capacity, internal resistance, voltage shape) taken before and after, with a rest in between, so we measure real wear and not noise.
Diesel-era analogy A structured dyno test: hold everything fixed, change one knob, measure precisely. That discipline is what later let engineers build the fuel map.
Three corrections you must make (or you'll measure noise) 1. The signal is tiny. The best real V2G study saw no measurable extra wear after 220 sessions over 3 years — both the V2G car and a parked control car lost ~8%, mostly from just sitting full (calendar aging). One 4-hour block changes capacity by thousandths of a percent — invisible in road kilometres. 2. Road range is noisy. Wind, payload, traffic, temperature and the driver each move range by several percent. 3. Warm ≠ worn. Right after V2G the pack is hot and "polarised"; an immediate re-drive differs for temporary reasons, not aging — so you must rest it first.
What you actually get Not "wear per block" (too small to see), but the stress signatures — efficiency, resistance growth, heat, rate — that let you calibrate a wear model and extrapolate to year 1 / 3 / 5 / 10, plus a "how many V2G sessions per day before you shorten the truck's life" curve. That is the valuable result.

A3 · The V2G wear map — the battery's "fuel-consumption map"

NEW — your ideaProduct-shaped deliverable
A single chart/surface that says: "at this power, this charge level and this temperature, V2G costs you this much battery life."
flowchart LR
    TEST["Controlled tests (A2): vary power, charge window, temperature"] --> FIT["Fit a wear model"]
    FIT --> MAP["WEAR MAP: wear = F(power, charge level, temperature, sessions)"]
    MAP --> U1["Charger picks a safe power & charge window"]
    MAP --> U2["Optimizer uses it as the 'cost of V2G'"]
    MAP --> U3["Warranty: how fast each session burns the budget"]
    MAP --> U4["'Max V2G sessions/day vs target lifetime' curve"]
In plain words Once you've run the tests, you fit a smooth map. After that, the charger never has to guess — it just looks up the map, exactly like an engine controller looks up the fuel map.
Why it matters This is the thing product planning and warranty teams can actually hold. It is the bridge between your physical test (A2) and every decision idea below.
Watch out A few days of testing covers only a few operating points. Be honest that the map is an extrapolation anchored by measurements, and state its confidence range — don't oversell it as a fully validated lifetime model.

Decision functions — the "new method" candidates

B1 · Warranty as a shadow-priced budget (the OEM-only headline)

Headline noveltyTRATON value: CriticalNeeds warranty terms
Treat the warranty MAN gives (e.g. "80% health guaranteed for X years or Y energy") as a bank balance that V2G spends — and compute the true price of spending it.
flowchart TB
    WARR["Warranty: health stays above 80% until X years or Y energy"] --> BUD["Remaining warranty budget"]
    BUD --> RULE["Rule: total V2G energy must stay within the budget"]
    PRICE["Possible V2G revenue today"] --> CHECK
    RULE --> CHECK{"Is today's revenue worth the warranty it burns?"}
    CHECK -->|yes| GO["Do V2G"]
    CHECK -->|no| HOLD["Hold back, protect the battery"]
    CHECK --> SP["By-product: the 'price of warranty' in cents per kWh"]
In plain words Everyone else asks "will V2G void my warranty?" from the owner's side. You sit at the company that writes the warranty. So you can ask the better question: if we treat the warranty as a spendable budget, what is each V2G session really worth, and what warranty product makes V2G a win for both MAN and the customer?
Why it matters It turns a vague worry into a number ("the price of warranty"), and it produces a real MAN artifact: "here is the V2G warranty we should sell, and why it makes money for everyone." Nobody outside an OEM can credibly write this.
Watch out You need MAN's actual warranty terms (or a representative version). Without them this stays theoretical. This is question #1 in "what I need from you."

B2 · Age-adaptive (life-stage) control — rules that change as the battery ages

Amended from another agent's ideaNovelty: the twist matters
Don't use one fixed rule per chemistry. Let the optimizer's rules themselves loosen as the battery gets older and, surprisingly, tougher.
flowchart TD
A["Truck plugs in"] --> B["Read chemistry + health + age"]
B --> C{"Life stage?"}
C -->|"Fresh battery"| D["Tight charge window, gentle rate"]
C -->|"Mid-life"| E["Gradually loosen window and rate"]
C -->|"Aged / tough chemistry (LFP)"| F["Wide window, deeper V2G allowed"]
D --> G["Use the wear map for cost"]
E --> G
F --> G
G --> H["Warranty check, then dispatch"]
In plain words A common idea is "NMC battery = careful, LFP = relaxed." That's just a fixed setting, and it's already well studied. The sharper idea: as a careful NMC battery ages, it actually becomes more tolerant of deep cycling — so the rules should change over the battery's life, not stay fixed.
Why it matters It lets old trucks earn more V2G money safely in their late life, instead of being retired early — directly relevant to fleet economics.
Watch out Plain "NMC vs LFP" comparison alone is not novel — the literature is full of it. The age-evolving rule set is the part worth writing about.

B3 · Per-truck value attribution — a "shadow-price bill"

Novelty: Medium-HighGreat product feature
Tell the fleet operator exactly which truck cost or earned what — information the optimizer already produces for free.
flowchart LR
    SOLVE["Optimizer solves the depot plan"] --> DUAL["It also produces a 'marginal cost' per truck (for free)"]
    DUAL --> MSG["'Truck 7 cost +23 euro: it arrived nearly empty and forced expensive peak charging'"]
    MSG --> U1["Driver incentives"]
    MSG --> U2["Smarter routing"]
    MSG --> U3["Where to add chargers"]
In plain words When the optimizer solves the schedule, it quietly computes how much each truck added to the bill. Normally that number is thrown away. Surface it, and the operator suddenly sees which truck and why.
Why it matters Same mathematics as the warranty price (B1), so it is almost free to add — and it is exactly the kind of feature that differentiates a charging product.
Watch out Too thin to be a thesis on its own; use it as the output layer of the unified thesis.

Markets & scale — bigger revenue, more trucks

C1 · Manufacture firm grid-service capacity by shaping departures

Novelty: HighTRATON value: Critical (revenue)
The grid pays well for guaranteed standby power. Trucks leave at random — so schedule them to always keep a guaranteed group plugged in.
flowchart TD
A["Routes, deadlines, driver hours"] --> B["Shape departures so a rotating group is ALWAYS plugged in"]
B --> C["Guaranteed standby power (reliable 99% of the time)"]
C --> D{"Charger type?"}
D -->|"Standard (CCS)"| E["Up to ~350 kW per truck"]
D -->|"Megawatt (MCS)"| F["Up to ~750 kW+ per truck"]
E --> G["Sell grid-balancing service, gated by warranty price"]
F --> G
G --> H["Compare: arbitrage-only vs arbitrage + grid services"]
In plain words Grid-balancing markets (like "FCR") pay a lot, but they demand that you promise a fixed amount of power for hours. A single random truck can't promise that. But a depot can rotate trucks so there's always a reliable group available — turning "we might be here" into "we guarantee it."
Why it matters Grid-balancing services are the majority of battery-storage revenue in Germany — far more than simple buy-low-sell-high. This is the literal product the Energisers team would sell.
Watch out Existing research treats truck availability as fixed and just bids around it. The novelty here is making availability a decision by shaping logistics — don't lose that distinction.

C2 · Multi-depot "virtual power plant"

Novelty: MediumRisk: needs multi-site data
Combine several depots so their ups and downs average out — together they can promise grid services that no single depot could.
flowchart TD
A["Depot 1"] --> D["Coordinator"]
B["Depot 2"] --> D
C["Depot n"] --> D
D --> E["Combined, smoother availability"]
E --> F["Decide market participation"]
F --> G["Grid service / arbitrage / peak shaving"]
G --> H["Share revenue and risk back to each depot"]
In plain words One depot is jumpy; five depots together are steady (the law of large numbers). Steady means you can make firm promises to the grid.
Watch out You need believable data from several sites. If you only have one, simulate the others from one depot's statistics, and ask "how many depots do we need?" — a legitimate and useful framing.

C3 · How much model do you actually need? (value-of-fidelity)

Novelty: HighOutput: firmware spec
Build the full detailed simulation, then switch parts off one by one to find which details actually change the decision — and which are just decoration.
flowchart LR
FULL["Full detailed model"] --> BASE["Best plan and profit"]
FULL --> ABL["Turn OFF one detail at a time"]
ABL --> A1["minus fine time resolution"]
ABL --> A2["minus detailed aging"]
ABL --> A3["minus uncertainty handling"]
A1 --> REG["How much worse is the decision?"]
A2 --> REG
A3 --> REG
REG --> SPEC["The simplest model the real MAN controller needs"]
In plain words Everyone keeps adding complexity. Almost nobody asks "did that complexity change any decision?" This idea answers exactly that — and the answer is a recipe for what the real on-truck controller must compute.
Why it matters It turns a simulation tool into a finding, and the finding is directly usable by MAN's software team.

Required by your jobReusable tool

flowchart LR

In plain words Instead of a hand-written table comparing two countries, build a small program that converts rules into settings automatically. Then it works for any future market too (India, Brazil, US).

D2 · Decision matrix for customers and governments

NEW — your ideaStrong results engine
Keep the battery and optimizer fixed; sweep truck type × mission × chemistry × country, and read off who wins where — for buyers and for regulators.
flowchart TB
FIX["Fixed: aging model + optimizer"] --> SWEEP["Sweep combinations (structured, not brute force): truck × mission × chemistry × country"]
SWEEP --> RUN["Run the platform for each"]
RUN --> O1["Customer matrix: best chemistry + strategy per mission/country"]
RUN --> O2["Government view: how much value each market design leaves unused"]
In plain words Once the platform is trustworthy, you run it across many situations and tabulate the winners. A customer sees "for my routes in my country, buy this battery and use this strategy." A regulator sees "our rules waste this much value compared to the neighbours."
Why it matters Two audiences, two concrete artifacts — sales support and policy evidence. It is the natural capstone that consumes everything else.
Watch out The combinations explode quickly. Use a structured experiment design (a sensible subset), not every possible mix.

Discussion chapter

flowchart TD

In plain words As wind and solar grow, power gets cheaper on average but swings more wildly. The value of V2G shifts from "buy cheap, sell dear" toward "catch the wild swings." This chapter stress-tests your method against that future.
Watch out Frame these as scenarios, not predictions. "Electricity will be free" is wrong; "more frequent near-zero-price windows" is the defensible version.

The real-truck test protocol (the rigorous version of your experiment)

This is the most actionable part — something you could hand to the MAN testing team. The principle: keep your intuitive drive test as the story, but measure with sensitive electrical metrics and control the temperature, so you capture real wear instead of noise.

StepWhat you doWhat you measure (the sensitive part)What you hold constant
1 · Reference (before)Controlled partial discharge in a fixed charge window Capacity (by counting energy), internal resistance (short current pulses), voltage-vs-charge shape Same temperature, battery fully rested
2 · Optional driveDrive a fixed route 60% → 50%Distance (your intuitive proxy) Same route, payload, weather, driver
3 · V2G block4-hour session at a chosen power, charge window, temperature Energy throughput, peak rate, average temperature, charge-level pathLog battery data every second
4 · RestLet temperature and voltage settle—Return to the reference temperature
5 · Reference (after)Repeat step 1 exactlySame metrics → the change in resistance and capacityIdentical to step 1
6 · Repeat & fitRepeat at different powers / windows / temperatures Fit the wear model; extrapolate to 1/3/5/10 yearsOne variable at a time
The payoff A wear map (A3) and a "max V2G sessions per day vs. target truck lifetime" curve — exactly what warranty and product planning need, and the credibility anchor for the whole thesis.
Reality check You will not see capacity fade accumulate in a few days — it's too slow. You are measuring the stress and resistance signatures that feed the model. Say this plainly in the thesis; it is a strength (honest method), not a weakness.

Honest cautions (so the thesis survives an examiner)

  • "A big simulation" is not a contribution. Heavy-truck digital twins already exist. The twin is your lab; the closed-loop passport→map→warranty→control function is the contribution.
  • Several popular angles are already published — NMC-vs-LFP comparison, MPC charging, chance-constrained grid-service bidding for cars. Use them as backbone, not headline.
  • You can't measure battery fade in days. Measure stress signatures and extrapolate; be explicit about confidence.
  • Don't predict "free electricity." The defensible future is "more frequent near-zero-price windows and higher volatility."

What I need from you to lock the final scope

  1. Warranty terms: can you get MAN's actual eTruck battery warranty (health threshold, year/energy limits)? The headline (B1) needs it — or a representative anonymised version.
  2. Test freedom: can you command arbitrary charge/discharge profiles, or only a fixed pilot script? Decides how rich the test (A2) can be.
  3. Data resolution: charger + truck logs at 1-second / 1-minute? Needed for the stress signatures.
  4. Pack history & passport: do you have age, mileage, throughput, current health for the test truck — and any access to a passport data model?
  5. In-house aging model: can you see/edit its equations, or is it a black box? Decides whether you tune it or wrap a correction around it.
  6. Deliverable emphasis: publishable method, internal MAN tool, or future product concept?

Answer these and I'll turn the unified thesis into a full formal proposal (objectives, method, data plan, timeline) — and write the test protocol as a standalone document for the MAN team.

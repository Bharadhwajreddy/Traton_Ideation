The problem, in plain words

An electric truck has a huge battery that mostly sits idle in the depot. V2G means using it to store cheap power and sell it back dear, or to support the grid — earning money while parked. The catch: every charge/discharge wears the battery a little.

The battery has one finite life. How do we spend that life across the most valuable money-making services, without breaking the warranty or the truck's day job — and how does the best answer change by country, mission and battery chemistry?

Bigger new ideas (you asked for better ones)

NEW 1 · The life-budget portfolio optimizer the spine

Treat battery life as a budget and the revenue services as investments competing for it — then solve the best portfolio, like an investor splitting money across assets.
flowchart LR
    BUD["ONE finite life-budget (kWh of wear before warranty limit)"] --> ALLOC{"How to spend it?"}
    WEAR["Wear map: life-cost of each service"] --> ALLOC
    ALLOC --> A["Arbitrage: low wear, low pay"]
    ALLOC --> F["FCR: standby, ? wear, high pay"]
    ALLOC --> AF["aFRR: medium wear, medium pay"]
    ALLOC --> PK["Peak shaving"]
    A --> MAX["Maximise TOTAL lifetime value"]
    F --> MAX
    AF --> MAX
    PK --> MAX
In plain words Most studies optimise one service, or treat wear as a flat "tax per kWh." Reality: the battery can only be cycled so much before the warranty limit, and several services compete for those cycles. This idea asks "given a fixed life budget, which mix of services earns the most over the truck's life?" — a portfolio problem where battery life is the scarce capital.
Why it's better & bigger It absorbs C1 (grid services become one asset in the portfolio), D1/D2 (the best portfolio differs by country/mission → that is the matrix), and A1–3 (the wear map prices each asset). One coherent, novel, 9-month contribution.
Diesel-era analogy A haulier with a fixed engine-overhaul interval choosing which jobs are worth the engine hours. You're doing that for battery cycles.

NEW 2 · Bankable firm grid-service capacity from a real fleet grows C1

Not "can a truck do FCR?" but "how many MW can a depot promise the grid for a whole year, given real wear, warranty, logistics and the market's 1-MW floor — and what does it do to truck cost of ownership?"
flowchart TB
    FLEET["Depot of N trucks (real logistics + arrivals)"] --> SHAPE["Shape departures: keep an always-present sub-fleet"]
    SHAPE --> AGG["Aggregate available power"]
    AGG --> FLOOR{"Reach the 1 MW market floor?"}
    FLOOR -->|"CCS ~0.35 MW each: need 3+ trucks"| BID
    FLOOR -->|"MCS ~0.75 MW each: need ~2 trucks"| BID
    BID["Bid firm FCR (4h blocks, weekdays only) and aFRR energy (25-min gate)"]
    BID --> CHK["Subtract wear + warranty cost (from wear map)"]
    CHK --> YR["Year-round bankable MW + cost-of-ownership impact per country"]
In plain words A grid operator only pays you if you can guarantee power. This idea works out how much a real depot can guarantee across a year — counting the days trucks are away, the wear cost, and the rule that you can't bid below 1 MW.
Why it matters This turns the small "C1 feasibility" question into a full business case with a number MAN can take to a customer: "your 12-truck depot can bank X MW and cut TCO by Y €/truck/year in Germany, Z in the Netherlands."
Fact — the market rules that make this concrete Germany FCR: minimum bid 1 MW (1 MW steps), 4-hour products (6/day), daily auction, capacity-only payment, symmetric ±, automatic activation. aFRR: 15-min delivery periods, energy bids accepted until 25 min before delivery (your "BSP bidding 25 min ahead" = aFRR, not FCR). One truck at CCS ≈ 0.35 MW < 1 MW floor → aggregation is mandatory.

NEW 3 · V2G vs. resale / second-life value — the hidden cost fresh angle

Every V2G session lowers the battery's health, which lowers its resale / second-life value — a cost almost nobody counts. Find the V2G intensity that maximises first-life revenue + leftover value.
flowchart LR
    V2G["More V2G now"] --> REV["More revenue today"]
    V2G --> SOH["Lower state of health"]
    SOH --> RES["Lower second-life / resale value (recorded in the passport)"]
    REV --> NET{"Total value = revenue today + leftover value"}
    RES --> NET
    NET --> OPT["Find the V2G intensity that maximises the TOTAL"]
In plain words A worn battery is worth less when the truck is sold or the pack goes to second life (e.g. stationary storage). So aggressive V2G isn't free even ignoring warranty — it eats resale value. This idea balances "earn now" against "keep value for later."
Why it's novel The battery passport exists precisely to record health for second-life markets — so connecting V2G intensity to passport-recorded residual value is a natural, timely, and under-explored question for trucks. Strong standalone chapter or a twist on the spine.

Kept & expanded — the ideas you liked

A1 · Battery passport as a live control input you liked this

Make the battery's lifecycle record something the charger reads and writes in real time, not a document filed for compliance.
flowchart TB
    PP["Battery passport (cloud): cycle history, age, temperature, state of health"] --> PLUG["Truck plugs in"]
    PLUG --> READ["Optimizer reads the passport"]
    READ --> DEC["Decide max power, charge window, V2G yes/no"]
    DEC --> RUN["Run the session"]
    RUN --> LOG["Measure throughput, rate, temperature, health"]
    LOG --> WRITE["Write the session back"]
    WRITE --> PP
In plain words The EU forces every big battery to carry a digital "passport" of its history from 2027. Today it's just a record. The idea: feed it into the charger's brain so an older/hotter battery automatically gets gentler treatment, and every session updates the record.
Fact EU Digital Battery Passport is mandatory from Feb 2027 for batteries > 2 kWh; DIN DKE SPEC 99100 (Feb 2025) defines the data, including state of health — but it updates only on "material change," not in real time. So you use the BMS for live control and the passport as the cross-charger, cross-owner record.

A2 · Your degradation test — and the test-bench question (good catch)

Your "drive 60→50%, do V2G, drive again, compare" is the right idea. A test bench makes it far better — but there's still one limit it can't remove, and one trick that fixes it.

Can a test bench / dyno see the difference you couldn't see on the road?

flowchart LR
    BENCH["Chassis dyno / battery test bench"] --> P1["Removes road noise: fixed load, repeatable, controlled temperature"]
    BENCH --> P2["Sensitive metrics: capacity by counting, internal resistance by current pulses, voltage-vs-charge shape"]
    BENCH --> P3["ACCELERATED aging: many cycles back-to-back, higher rate, warm -> force MEASURABLE wear in days/weeks"]
    P1 --> FIT["Fit the wear model, then scale back to normal conditions"]
    P2 --> FIT
    P3 --> FIT
Yes — three things the bench buys you 1. It removes the road noise (wind, traffic, payload, driver), so the same charge window gives a repeatable result — your instinct is correct. 2. Time/energy becomes a clean measurement: at a fixed load, the time from 60%→50% is directly proportional to usable energy, so a drop is meaningful once the noise is gone. 3. The big one — accelerated aging: on a bench you can run many cycles back-to-back at higher rate and temperature to force measurable fade in your time window, then scale the result back to normal use. This is how you actually get truck-pack degradation data in 9 months.
The one limit the bench can't remove Under normal conditions, a few V2G blocks still wear the pack by only thousandths of a percent — too small to see even on a bench. The bench helps by (a) measuring internal resistance, which grows much earlier and is far more sensitive than capacity, and (b) letting you accelerate. So: don't try to read fade off normal-use kilometres; read resistance growth, and accelerate to get real fade points.
Still control for "warm ≠ worn" Right after V2G the pack is hot and polarised; let it rest to a fixed temperature before the "after" measurement, or you'll measure temporary effects, not aging. (On a bench, temperature control makes this easy.)

C1 · Grid services — with the facts you asked for

You're right that "C1 as feasibility" is closer to a literature review than a thesis. So we keep it as the revenue engine inside new idea 1 / new idea 2, and arm it with the real market rules:

Question you askedAnswer (Germany / EU)
Minimum unit you can bid as an FCR provider?1 MW, in 1 MW steps (not 10 MW). 4-hour products, 6 per day, daily auction.
How is FCR paid / activated?Capacity payment only (€/MW), symmetric ±, automatic response within seconds — there is no activation-price bid.
Your "BSP bidding activation price 25 min before"?That's aFRR (and mFRR): 15-min delivery periods, energy bids accepted until 25 min before delivery. aFRR pays capacity and activated energy.
Can we offer only on weekdays / chosen hours?Yes — you bid per 4-hour block, so you can offer only the blocks/days your trucks are reliably present.
Is this possible for small amounts of energy?Not below 1 MW. One CCS truck ≈ 0.35 MW; one MCS truck ≈ 0.75 MW → you must aggregate ≈3 (CCS) or ≈2 (MCS) trucks to clear the floor, more for a firm year-round product.
How this becomes a thesis, not a literature review The research is not "what are the rules" — it's: given these rules + real wear cost + real truck availability, how much firm capacity can a depot bank across a year, and is it worth the battery life it costs? That is new idea 2, and it is genuinely thesis-sized.

D2 · Decision matrix for customers & governments your notebook idea

Keep the aging model + optimizer fixed; sweep vehicle mission × vehicle type × cell chemistry × country; read off who wins where — advice for customers, evidence for governments.
flowchart TB
    FIX["Fixed: same aging model + same optimizer"] --> SWEEP["Sweep (structured, not brute force): mission x vehicle type x chemistry x country"]
    SWEEP --> RUN["Run the platform for each combination"]
    RUN --> O1["Customer: which chemistry + truck + strategy is best for my mission & country"]
    RUN --> O2["Government: how much V2G value each country's rules leave on the table"]
In plain words (this is exactly your handwritten note) Hold the "engine" (aging + optimizer) constant, change the inputs — vehicle mission, vehicle type, chemistry, country — and tabulate the winner each time. A customer gets a buy/operate recommendation; a regulator sees how their market compares.
Why it's the perfect capstone It consumes everything else: it needs the wear map (A3), the optimizer (new 1), the grid-service rules (C1), and the country profiles (D1). That's why it works as the thesis output, not a separate small task.
Watch out The combinations explode. Use a structured experiment design (a sensible subset of missions/chemistries/countries), not every possible mix.

The literature you asked for — cars vs. trucks

This is the evidence base. Note the key story: for cars the findings contradict each other depending on how smart the control is; for trucks there is very little, and it suggests V2G can roughly double capacity fade if done carelessly — which is exactly the gap your wear map fills.

That's your thesis.

Honest cautions

  • A big simulation isn't the contribution — the measured wear map + the life-budget portfolio function is.
  • You can't read fade off normal-use kilometres — use internal resistance + accelerated aging on the bench.
  • Grid services need ≥1 MW — a single truck can't bid; aggregation is part of the problem, not an afterthought.
  • Truck V2G degradation evidence is thin and simulation-based — your measured map is genuinely new, so be rigorous and state confidence ranges.

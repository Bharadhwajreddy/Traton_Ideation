How the German electricity market
actually works

There is a market operator that clears day-ahead and intraday. There is a TSO that does balancing. There is a company offering to “be your BRP”. Those three sentences are all true and none of them tells you how the pieces fit together.

This page starts from the one physical fact that forces all of it to exist, explains what ancillary services actually are and how balancing sits inside them, then traces a single megawatt-hour from the forward market to the monthly invoice — naming every counterparty and quoting the clause or the formula behind each claim. No prior knowledge assumed.

Section 1 · start here

What the grid actually needs, and who sells it

Before any of the market machinery makes sense, it helps to know what problem it is all solving. There is only one, and it is brutally simple.

At every instant, the amount of electricity going into the grid must equal the amount coming out. Not on average over the day. Not roughly. At every instant.

Electricity is not stored in the wires. If consumption exceeds generation, the shortfall is taken out of the rotating mass of every generator on the system, they slow down, and the grid frequency falls below 50 Hz. If generation exceeds consumption, frequency rises. Drift far enough — roughly ±0.2 Hz before things get serious, ±2.5 Hz before equipment starts disconnecting itself — and the system begins to come apart.

Two very different jobs follow from that one sentence, and almost all the confusion in this area comes from mixing them up.

Job 1

Get the plan roughly right, in advance

Thousands of buyers and sellers agree in advance who will produce what and who will consume what, slot by slot. By the time delivery arrives, the plan already balances on paper.

This is the energy market — the day-ahead auction and the intraday market. It is a normal commodity market: you buy a thing, you pay for the thing, in €/MWh.

Job 2

Fix whatever the plan got wrong, in real time

No plan is ever right. Wind forecasts miss. A power station trips. A truck comes back from its shift 40 minutes late. Somebody has to hold equipment on standby, under contract, ready to move within seconds.

This is where ancillary services live. And it is a different kind of purchase: you are not buying energy, you are buying capability.

So what exactly is an “ancillary service”?

An ancillary service is anything the system operator needs in order to run the grid that is not the selling of energy itself. The EU definition (Directive 2019/944, Art. 2(48)) is deliberately broad: “a service necessary for the operation of a transmission or distribution system”.

an analogy

An airline sells you a seat. That is its product, and it is what appears on your ticket. But it cannot fly without air-traffic control, de-icing crews, a standby aircraft at the hub, ground power units and a fire service on the runway. Nobody buys a ticket for de-icing. It still has to exist, someone still has to be under contract to provide it, and someone still has to pay for it.

Energy is the seat. Ancillary services are everything else that makes the flight possible.

They are not variations on a theme. They solve genuinely different physical problems, and — this is the part that matters — they are bought in genuinely different ways.

Now the actual question: is the balancing market the same as the ancillary services market?

This question gets asked constantly and the honest answer has three parts. The short version is at the bottom if you want to skip ahead.

a · Balancing is inside ancillary services, not beside them

They are not two categories to compare. Balancing is family 1 of the five above. Every balancing product (FCR, aFRR, mFRR) is an ancillary service. Most ancillary services are not balancing.

So the relationship is containment, not rivalry — the same relationship as “cardiology” to “medicine”. Asking how they differ is asking the wrong shape of question.

b · There is no such thing as “the ancillary services market”

This is the part that actually resolves the confusion. “Ancillary services” is a category, not a marketplace. Look down the fourth column of the table above: one family is auctioned, one is a compliance obligation, one is negotiated bilaterally, one is not bought at all, and one is a legal instruction you cannot refuse.

There is no single venue, no single price, no single contract, and no single set of participants. Balancing is the only family of the five that is organised as a competitive market in Germany.

put another way

Asking “how does the ancillary services market differ from the balancing market?” is like asking how the transport market differs from the taxi market. The taxi market is a real, definable thing with prices and drivers you can hail. “The transport market” is an umbrella containing taxis, buses, freight rail and bicycles — organised in completely different ways, with different regulators and different money. You cannot buy a ticket for “transport”.

c · Two things make balancing unusual within the family

This is the “is there anything additional in balancing?” part of your question. Yes — two things, and neither has any equivalent in the other four families.

  1. It is split into two separate markets for the same physical thing. You are paid once in €/MW for standing ready (balancing capacity), and again in €/MWh if you are actually called (balancing energy). Two auctions, two prices, two merit orders, one asset. Nothing else in ancillary services works that way — a black-start contract is a single retainer, and reactive power is simply a condition of being connected.
  2. It has a financial twin that applies to everybody. Balancing is the only family that produces an imbalance price — the reBAP — which is charged to or paid to every single market participant, whether or not they have ever sold an ancillary service in their life. Nobody is ever “exposed to the black-start price”. Everybody, always, is exposed to the imbalance price.

What this means if someone offers you “ancillary services revenue”

In Germany, for a truck depot, the only genuinely sellable ancillary services are the three balancing products — FCR, aFRR and mFRR. Voltage support and black start are not open to you. Inertia is not bought. Redispatch is something that happens to you. So when a vendor says “ancillary services revenue”, the correct follow-up question is: which product, which direction, and which of the three payments are you quoting? If they cannot answer that in one sentence, the number is not real.

The three sentences worth memorising

  1. Ancillary services = everything the TSO needs that is not the selling of energy. It is a category, not a marketplace.
  2. Balancing = the frequency slice of that category. It is the one slice that really is a competitive market — in fact two markets (capacity and energy), plus a settlement process (imbalance) that catches everyone.
  3. Most of the rest of the category is not a market at all — it is obligation, bilateral contract, or a legal instruction you cannot refuse.

Section 3

The three payments — and why there have to be three

The same megawatt-hour can be paid for three times, under three contracts, with two different counterparties. That sounds like a swindle until you see what each payment is actually for. Then it becomes obvious.

the intuition, in one picture

Think of a town that contracts a private fire brigade.

  • The retainer. The town pays a fixed monthly fee just to have the crew sitting in the station, trained, with the engine fuelled. They are paid this whether or not there is a fire. That is balancing capacity.
  • The callout. When there is actually a fire, the brigade is paid again — per hour on site, for the water and the crew. Only if they are called. That is balancing energy.
  • The insurance excess. Completely separately, if your building was the one that caught fire because you left a heater on, you pay toward the damage — regardless of whether you have any relationship with the fire brigade at all. That is imbalance settlement.

The first two go to the people with the equipment. The third is charged to whoever caused the problem. They are unrelated contracts that happen to concern the same event.

The technical role. Owns or controls equipment that can change setpoint on command. Must pass prequalification with the TSO. These two payments are rewards for being useful — you bid, you win, you deliver.

The financial role. Owns nothing. This is not a reward and not a penalty — it is a price. It reprices your deviation at what energy was actually worth at that moment, in whichever direction the deviation pointed.

Why it matters

A pitch quoting “€/MW of aFRR” and a business case quoting “imbalance savings” are describing different contracts with different counterparties. Adding them together without checking is how flexibility business cases get built twice on the same megawatt.

A worked example: one Tuesday at Depot GmbH

Ten electric trucks, a 1.6 MW grid connection. The depot sits inside a service BRP’s balance group and has joined an aggregator’s prequalified aFRR pool. Here is every euro that moves, and who it moves between. Watch how the three payments never touch each other.

Payment 1 · capacity+ €48.00

At 09:00 on Monday the aggregator bids 1 MW of negative aFRR — a promise that the depot can charge harder than planned on command — for the 00:00–04:00 block on Tuesday. The bid clears. The depot has now sold an option.

1 MW × 4 hours × 12 €/MW·h = €48.00

Paid by the TSO to the BSP (the aggregator), who passes on its share. Paid whether or not the depot is ever called. If Tuesday night is uneventful, this €48 is the entire revenue — and the depot did nothing but keep chargers free.

Payment 2 · energy+ €5.10 net

At 01:15 Tuesday the grid is over-supplied — too much wind. PICASSO calls the pool for negative aFRR. The depot’s chargers ramp up for half an hour, absorbing 0.3 MWh more than planned.

0.3 MWh absorbed at the negative-direction marginal price of 45 €/MWh → the depot pays €13.50 for that energy.
But it would otherwise have bought those 0.3 MWh on the intraday market at 62 €/MWh = €18.60.
Net benefit = 18.60 − 13.50 = €5.10

This is the bit people get backwards. In the negative direction you are being paid to consume, so the cash flow looks like buying energy cheaply rather than like receiving a cheque. The energy is also neutralised in the balance group, so it does not show up as an imbalance — you were told to do it.

Payment 3 · imbalance− €8.80

Entirely separately: at 16:45 a truck comes back from its shift 40 minutes late with a lower state of charge than forecast. To make its 06:00 departure the next morning it has to start charging immediately. The depot draws 0.08 MWh more in that quarter hour than its schedule said.

The balance group is short by 0.08 MWh · the reBAP that quarter hour is 110 €/MWh · positive price + short group → you pay
0.08 MWh × 110 €/MWh = €8.80

Nobody called you. Nobody bought anything from you. The TSO simply observed that your group promised one thing and did another, and priced the difference at what energy was worth at that instant. This payment would have happened even if the depot had never heard of the balancing market.

Three lines, three contracts, one Tuesday. Payments 1 and 2 arrive through the aggregator acting as BSP. Payment 3 arrives as a line on the BRP’s monthly invoice. They are settled by different processes, on different timescales, under different agreements — and a depot can be exposed to payment 3 while having no relationship at all with payments 1 and 2.

Payment 3 is the one that behaves strangely

Payments 1 and 2 are intuitive: do a thing, get paid. Payment 3 is the one that catches people out, because it can go either way. Whether you pay or get paid depends on two signs — the sign of the price, and the sign of your own deviation.

reBAPYour balance groupResult
positiveshort — drew more than promisedyou pay
positivelong — drew less than promisedyou are paid
negativeshortyou are paid
negativelongyou pay

Why the sign flip is the whole point

A positive reBAP means the system was short and energy was scarce at that instant. If you took extra, you made it worse — you pay. If you took less than promised, you helped — you are paid.

A negative reBAP means the system was over-supplied and the TSO was paying people to consume. Now the logic inverts: taking extra helped, so you are paid; taking less made it worse, so you pay.

The price is not measuring how badly you forecast. It is measuring what your error did to the system. Get that one idea and section 10 — the one-price versus two-price argument — writes itself.

Section 4

The timeline: what happens when, and who is in charge at each moment

One delivery day, from a week out to the invoice. The thing to hold onto is that the markets and the balancing do not happen one after the other — they overlap.

the whole timeline as one analogy

You book a restaurant table.

  • Day-ahead auction — you reserve a table for four at 8 pm, at tonight’s advertised price.
  • Nomination — you confirm the booking with the restaurant. Now they have staffed and bought food for four covers. This is the step that makes it real.
  • Intraday — at 6 pm you call: make it five. They can still adjust, but the kitchen has already shopped, so the fifth cover costs a bit more than the first four did.
  • Delivery — you turn up with six people.
  • Imbalance settlement — the restaurant charges you for the two extra covers at whatever the walk-in rate was that night. And here is the twist: if they were over-booked and turning people away, the extra covers were a problem and you pay a premium. If the place was half empty, your two extras helped — and in the electricity version of this story you would actually be paid for them.

The restaurant never takes over your booking. It fixes the evening, then bills you for the difference between what you promised and what you did. That is exactly the relationship between you and the TSO.

Look at the two green bars on the right. The TSO is activating reserves all day long — and at the same time, the blue bar shows you are still trading. They run in parallel. There is no moment where one hands over to the other.

Activation automatic and continuous

Who runs it: physics, then the TSOs’ control systems, then the European platforms — in that order.

No energy moves at this stage — it is pure arithmetic about something that already happened.

Three things people say that are not quite right

“You can’t trade after H-1.”
Inside Germany you can trade until the quarter hour minus 5 minutes. Liquidity thins and the spread widens, but the market is open.
“The TSO starts balancing at H-1.”
It bought the capacity at 08:00–10:00 the previous morning, and FCR has been acting every second since midnight. Balancing never starts, because it never stops.
“The TSO takes over your position.”
It never assumes your obligation. It is the balancer of last resort, not the counterparty of last resort. It fixes the system, then bills your BRP for your share of the error.

Why the TSO’s job is smaller than it sounds

At any moment, hundreds of balance groups are out of position — some short, some long. Before the TSO does anything, those errors cancel against each other. A supplier whose customers used less than forecast offsets a wind farm that produced less than forecast.

The TSO only ever has to cover the net residual. This is why the total volume settled as imbalance energy is routinely far larger than the balancing energy actually activated — and why “how much balancing energy did Germany use” and “how much imbalance was there” are two very different questions with two very different answers.

Contractual aggregation Germany today

Prequalification requires a written certificate of knowledge and consent from the connecting grid operator, the supplier, the BRP and the operator wherever those roles are not the same legal person.

Consequence: an aggregator in Germany cannot activate your depot over the head of your supplier and your BRP. Expect to move your supply contract, or to get your supplier to sign.

Independent aggregation the EU target model

Article 17 of Directive (EU) 2019/944 requires Member States to enable a BSP acting over resources belonging to another BRP, with automatic imbalance adjustment and a compensation payment between the parties.

Not implemented in German balancing. The TSOs expect the EU Network Code on Demand Response to force it — ACER submitted it on 7 March 2025; not adopted as of early 2026. The Nordics are building it through eSett. This is a live regulatory question.

Section 6

What “contractual aggregation” means

The phrase appears in every European flexibility document and is almost never explained. It is a simple idea about whose permission you need.

Your depot alone is too small to sell flexibility to a TSO. An aggregator signs up many sites, pools them and sells the combination — and to do that it needs to change what your chargers do, remotely, without asking you each time. That is where the problem starts.

Your electricity is already somebody else’s business.

You buy from a supplier, who sits in a BRP’s balance group, and that BRP has already promised the TSO exactly what your meter will draw tomorrow. If a third party makes your trucks charge harder at 01:15, your supplier has to buy energy it never planned to sell you, and your BRP’s balance group goes short and pays the imbalance price. Neither agreed to it; neither is paid for it.

the analogy

You drive a company car with a fuel card your employer pays for. A delivery firm offers you money to run evening errands — but the fuel comes off your employer’s card.

  • Contractual aggregation — the delivery firm must get your employer’s written agreement first. If your employer says no, the deal is dead.
  • Independent aggregation — the law lets the firm hire you anyway, and a central process automatically bills it for the fuel and credits your employer.

That is the whole distinction.

Germany does the first one

It is not a vague norm but a hard prequalification requirement: before a unit can enter the balancing market, the applicant must file a certificate of knowledge and consent (Kenntnis- und Einverständniserklärung) from every market role involved that is not the same legal person.

Whose signatureWhy they get a veto
The connecting grid operatorThe flexibility changes flows on their network
Your supplierTheir sales volume to you changes without their agreement
Your BRPTheir nominated schedule becomes wrong, exposing them to the imbalance price
The operator of the asset (you)It is your equipment and your operations at risk
The practical consequence. Your supplier can simply refuse to sign, and refusing costs them nothing. That is why most German flexibility offers arrive as a bundle: the aggregator is also the supplier and the BRP, so all the signatures are their own. If you look at an aggregator that is not your supplier, the first question is not “what is the revenue share?” but “has my supplier agreed to sign?”
Why the signature matters mechanically. Because the BRP consented, the TSO knows an activated volume was its own instruction, and neutralises it in the balance group — the schedule is settled as if it had always included that energy, so the BRP pays nothing and the aggregator is paid. No signature, no neutralisation, and the whole thing collapses into someone else’s imbalance bill.

Independent aggregation — where Europe is heading

Article 17 of Directive (EU) 2019/944 requires Member States to let an aggregator act without the supplier’s or BRP’s agreement — otherwise your supplier can veto every rival and there is no flexibility market, only flexibility bundled into supply contracts. Two mechanisms replace the signature: an automatic imbalance adjustment that removes the delivered volume from the host BRP’s position, and a centrally calculated compensation payment between aggregator and supplier.

Status. Not implemented in German balancing. The TSOs expect the EU Network Code on Demand Response to force it — ACER submitted it to the Commission on 7 March 2025, and it had not been adopted as of early 2026. The Nordics are building it through eSett. Re-check before citing; this is a genuinely open question.

The one-line version. Contractual aggregation = you need everyone’s permission first. Independent aggregation = the law gives you access and pays them off automatically. Germany is the first; until the second lands, your supplier holds a veto over who may monetise your chargers.

Section 7

“We’ll be your BRP” — what is actually being sold

And the question everybody asks: do they just forward me the invoice, or do they ever pay?

first, the basic

A Bilanzkreis (balance group) is a bank account for electricity. Everything fed in is a deposit; everything consumed or sold on is a withdrawal. The rule is that the account must balance to exactly zero in every 15-minute period — not on average, every period. The BRP is the account holder, and if the account does not balance, the TSO squares it and charges a price set after the fact: the reBAP.

Under § 4 StromNZV every meter in Germany must sit in some balance group at all times — your depot is in one right now, almost certainly your supplier’s. There is no unassigned electricity. What you are buying below is somebody else’s account, and somebody else’s obligation to keep it flat.

The bundle

  1. Access. Their Bilanzkreis, their EIC, their contract with the TSO — so you need none of those, and no collateral with the TSO.
  2. Nomination. Your schedule by 14:30 D-1 and your intraday changes, in the correct EDI@Energy format, to the right TSO.
  3. Market access. Often exchange membership too, for day-ahead and intraday execution.
  4. Risk absorption. How much — entirely contractual. See the table.
  5. Settlement handling. They receive the TSO’s monthly invoice for the whole balance group and re-allocate it.

first, one word of jargon

“The tail” means the rare, extreme, expensive day. It comes from the shape of a probability chart: most outcomes bunch up in the middle, and the thin stretched-out ends — the tails — are the rare ones. In this context the tail is the quarter hour where the imbalance price goes berserk.

Most days your deviation costs you a few euros. But in a genuine scarcity quarter hour the price can reach 19 998 €/MWh — so being short by a quarter of a megawatt-hour for fifteen minutes costs about €5 000. That single quarter hour is the tail.

So “who carries the tail?” simply means: on that one terrible day, whose money is it? It is the same question as car insurance. Nothing happens in most months; the tail is the crash. You can choose to carry it yourself and pay nothing in the meantime, or pay a premium every month so that somebody else carries it.

“Are there only three?” — no

There are not three models. There are about six independent dials, and a contract is wherever somebody set them.

The three above are archetypes — common combinations, chosen because they sit far apart and make the trade-off visible. Real offers mix and match, and two contracts with the same name can behave completely differently.

Think of each row below as a slider. Every contract sets all six of them somewhere, and it is the combination that makes one offer different from another — not a label on the front page.

Section 8

Buying the power: what happens in the auction

The question asked almost word for word every time: “we don’t know the prices before we bid — so what exactly are we doing?”

You do not submit a price. You submit how much you want at every possible price.

A day-ahead bid is not “1.6 MW at 70 €/MWh”. It is a curve: “for 02:00–02:15 I will take 1.6 MW at or below 70; 0.8 MW between 70 and 120; nothing above 120.” You declare your willingness to pay, not a guess at the outcome.

Every buyer submits a curve like that and every seller the mirror image. The exchange collects them all in secret — this is why it is a blind auction, nobody ever sees anyone else’s bids — stacks them into one demand curve and one supply curve, and finds where they cross.

Uniform pricing

Everyone who clears pays the same price — where the curves cross, not what they bid. So a high bid limit buys certainty, never expense.

It is blind

You submit by 12:00, the algorithm clears all coupled European zones at once, results appear around 12:45. No haggling, no second round.

96 separate clearings

Since 1 October 2025 the German auction clears one product per quarter hour, each with its own price. Your bid curve is per quarter hour too.

So what do you actually submit?

Option 1 · price-dependent

“Charge in this quarter hour only if it is cheap enough.” This is what flexible demand is supposed to do — your bid tells the market what your flexibility is worth.

The catch: if the price comes in above your limit you get nothing that quarter hour. Do that too often and your trucks do not charge.

Option 2 · price-taker, against a forecast

“Forecast tomorrow’s prices, pick the cheapest windows, then bid to clear for certain.” You are guaranteed the energy; you are not guaranteed to have picked the right hours.

This is what almost everyone does. The skill is not in the bidding, it is in the forecast.

How the simulator simplifies this. “Cheapest hours” optimises against the day’s actual cleared prices — a perfect forecast. Real life is option 2 with a worse one, so treat the simulator’s arbitrage saving as an upper bound, not an estimate.

And what is the BRP doing?

They decide — full supply

The BRP forecasts you from history, buys what it thinks you need, nominates it and bills you at your agreed tariff. You are not involved in the auction at all and may never see a day-ahead price — and there is nothing for flexible charging to capture.

You decide — they execute the simulator’s model

You send a charging plan by their cut-off (often 13:00). They place the order on the exchange in your name — you are not an exchange member — and nominate the matching schedule to the TSO by 14:30. The decision is yours; the access is theirs.

What the BRP always does, either way: turn the trade into a nomination. A trade is a contract with a counterparty; a nomination is a promise to the system operator. Until 14:30 D-1 your purchase is paperwork between traders — after it, the TSO has built its reserve plan around your quarter hours.

Intraday is not another auction

Continuous intraday works like a share market: a live order book where a trade happens the instant two offers overlap. So there is a bid–ask spread — you buy at the ask and sell at the bid, and every round trip costs you the gap before any fee. Liquidity thins as delivery approaches, so the spread widens exactly when you most need to trade. You can deal until 5 minutes before the quarter hour inside Germany.

Section 9

The reBAP — the price of getting it wrong

reBAP stands for regelzonenübergreifender einheitlicher Bilanzausgleichsenergiepreis — literally “the single balancing-energy price that applies across all control zones”. One number, per quarter hour, for the whole of Germany.

What is actually being priced here

Rewind fifteen minutes. In that quarter hour, hundreds of balance groups across Germany were slightly wrong: some drew more electricity than they had promised, some drew less. Added together, Germany as a whole ended up either short (consumed more than it generated) or long (generated more than it consumed).

The grid cannot tolerate that, so the TSO fixed it in real time — it telephoned, or rather signalled, the power stations and batteries that were on standby and told them to produce more, or to produce less. That costs money, and somebody has to pay for it. The reBAP is how that cost is turned into a price and charged to the people who caused it.

In one sentence: the reBAP is “what did it cost to clean up this quarter hour?” — with two guard rails bolted on so that nobody can profit from causing the mess.

The three modules, in plain words

German regulation calculates three candidate prices for every quarter hour, then picks one. Each candidate answers a different question.

Module 1 · “what did it actually cost?”

The real, measured price the TSO paid the plants it called. If it needed more power, this is what the last and most expensive megawatt-hour of it cost. This is the honest answer, and most of the time it is the one that wins.

Module 2 · “don’t let anyone game it”

Sometimes fixing the imbalance was cheap — so if the price were left at Module 1, deliberately going short would be cheaper than buying your energy properly on the market. Everyone would do it, and the system would fall apart.

So Module 2 takes the market price at that moment and adds a margin on top. It is a floor when the system is short and a ceiling when it is long, and its only job is to make sure that being imbalanced is never the cheap option.

Module 3 · “we are running out”

Does nothing at all until the imbalance is so big that Germany has used up 80 % of everything it holds in reserve. Past that point the price climbs steeply — not because the last megawatt-hour cost that much, but as a deliberate alarm signal. It is meant to be rare, and on most days it never fires.

Then the rule that picks one of the three

If Germany was short → take the highest of the three, so being short is always painful.
If Germany was long → take the lowest of the three, so being long is never rewarding.

That is the whole calculation. Everything below is just this, with the real numbers in it.

Imbalance revenue and balancing cost do not match. The TSOs sum the annual deficit or surplus and roll it into network tariffs (NNE) — which makes them financially neutral and shifts the residual onto network users.

One price or two: the argument, settled

Germany and the Nordics both use single pricing. About five EU TSOs still use dual. Here is why, with the honest objection and what the systems actually do about it.

Day-ahead 30 €/MWh, imbalance 33 when short and 27 when long. Read the last two columns as incentives. On the aggravating rows the two schemes are identical. They differ only on the helping rows — and there, two-price replaces a reward with the day-ahead price, i.e. with indifference.

Why single pricing won

  1. Direction, not magnitude. Two-price penalises deviation as such; one-price prices its effect on the system.
  2. Passive balancing is free reserve. Every MW BRPs supply by leaning the right way is a MW the TSO does not activate — and, through dimensioning, eventually a MW it does not procure.
  3. The imbalance price should equal the real-time value of energy. Two-price deliberately breaks that for half the market in every period.
  4. The revenue is a transfer. Under financial neutrality it is recycled anyway, so two-price functions as a tax on forecast error — falling hardest on variable renewables and small portfolios.
  5. It distorts intraday. Facing a spread against the imbalance price, a BRP trades at prices it knows to be bad purely to avoid it. Noise, not price discovery.
  6. It is the EU legal default. EB GL Art. 55 sets single pricing as the model; dual requires explicit approval.

The honest objection — and the real fix

Single pricing does create a speculative incentive to under- or over-nominate if you can forecast the system’s direction. The textbook remedy — cap the imbalance price back at the day-ahead price — is two-price, and throws out the cooperation incentive with the speculation incentive.

What real systems do instead:

  • Module 2. Forces a minimum spread against the intraday index, scaled by the system imbalance. Imbalance is then never cheaper than trading flat, in either direction, at all times.
  • Module 3. In tight periods the price escalates far beyond anything a speculator would risk.
  • Ziffer 5.2. Bilanzkreistreue is a contractual duty. Deliberate systematic imbalance is investigable under Ziffer 11.4, with REMIT on top.
  • 15-minute periods. They shrink both the structural imbalance and the window in which anyone forecasts the system better than the market.
The clean formulation. Single pricing with incentivising and scarcity components dominates dual pricing, because it preserves the property people want from dual pricing — imbalance is never cheaper than trading — without destroying the cooperation signal.

Two German rules about network charges — and which one is yours

This trips up almost every EV-charging business case, so it is worth five slow paragraphs.

§ 14a EnWG — not yours

This is the rule everyone quotes. It says: if you install a big controllable device — a home wallbox, a heat pump, a battery — the grid operator may briefly throttle it at moments of local stress, and in exchange you get a discount on your network charges.

It applies only if all three are true:

  • the device draws more than 4.2 kW, and
  • it is connected in low voltage — the 400 V wiring a house or small shop is on, and
  • it was commissioned from 1 January 2024 onwards.

A depot pulling 1.6 MW is not on low voltage. It sits on a medium-voltage connection with its own transformer and a meter that records your load every 15 minutes (RLM). So § 14a simply does not reach you. If a vendor's business case leans on it, that number is not real.

§ 19(2) StromNEV — this one is yours

The logic here is different, and rather elegant. Network charges exist to pay for a grid that has to be built big enough for the worst moment of the year — the evening in February when everyone draws at once.

Every network operator publishes the hours in which that worst moment historically falls. They are called Hochlastzeitfenster — “high-load time windows”. Typically a few hours on winter weekday evenings; the exact windows are your operator’s, and published.

The deal: if your own yearly peak reliably lands outside those windows, you are not one of the reasons the grid had to be built that big — so you can be charged individually instead of on the standard tariff. The reduction can be large; the rule allows an individual charge down to as little as 20 % of the standard rate.

Two conditions, and both are hard:

  • The arrangement must be notified to the regulator by 30 September of the year it is to apply to. Miss the date and you wait a year.
  • You must actually stay out of the windows — which means your charging controller has to treat them as a hard no every single day, not a preference it drops when a truck is late.
Why this matters more than anything else on this page. A depot is the near-perfect candidate: its trucks sit still overnight, and overnight is exactly when the high-load windows are not. Moving your peak out of those windows is a software decision, not an investment — and it can beat every energy-trading strategy in this document put together.
Note for accuracy. An earlier version of this page named § 14a EnWG as the depot’s grid-charge lever. That was wrong, for the reason above, and it is corrected here. The other branch of § 19(2) — intensive Netznutzung, which needs at least 7 000 full-load hours and 10 GWh a year — is far beyond a truck depot and can be ignored.

Can you be your own BRP?

There is no licence and no prequalification — it is a contract, not a permit. But for ten trucks (order of magnitude 6 MWh/day) it is economically absurd: the collateral formula, a 24/7 schedule desk and EDI@Energy capability do not scale down. The realistic structures are:

  1. Supplier with a dynamic tariff. Zero effort, zero exposure, almost none of the value.
  2. Service BRP + your own Unterbilanzkreis. You keep the trading decisions and the upside; they keep the TSO relationship and the collateral. This is the structure the simulator models.
  3. Aggregator as BSP. Your depot joins someone else’s prequalified pool — with your supplier’s and BRP’s written consent.
  4. Own BSP prequalification. Only at pool scale. 10 × 350 kW is nominally 3.5 MW; reliably available is a much smaller, time-varying number.
Section 12

Becoming a BRP yourself: the rules, and who is allowed

Everything above assumed you rent the role. This section is the other answer: what the law actually requires if you want to hold it yourself, and who is eligible.

There is no licence. Being a BRP is a contract, not a permission.

Germany does not issue “BRP authorisations”. You become a Bilanzkreisverantwortlicher by signing a Bilanzkreisvertrag (balance group contract) with a transmission system operator — and under § 26 StromNZV the TSO and the applicant are obliged to conclude that contract, on the standard terms the regulator has approved. The TSO cannot pick and choose.

Who is eligible

Anyone who can meet the contract’s conditions. A BRP is a purely financial role — it owns no power stations and no cables — so there is no technical qualification and no energy-sector licence involved. In practice the balance groups in Germany are held by suppliers, traders, generators, direct marketers, large industrial consumers, and financial institutions. A depot operator is not excluded by anything except economics.

The actual steps

These are the steps the German TSOs publish. Details differ slightly between the four, so check the page of the TSO whose control area you sit in — the example below follows Amprion’s published process.

1 · Decide which control areas you need

A balance group exists inside one control area. Germany has four TSOs — 50Hertz, Amprion, TenneT DE and TransnetBW — so a nationwide BRP needs at least one balance group, and one contract, in each. A single depot only needs the one its meter sits in.

2 · Get an EIC code

The Energy Identification Code is the unique 16-character identifier every balance group trades under. In Germany it is requested from and issued by the BDEW. Nothing can be registered without it.

3 · Register with the TSO as market role “BKV”

Each TSO runs a portal for market participants — Amprion’s is the Netzwirtschaftsportal. You register there under the market role BKV.

4 · Request a contract offer, in writing

A formal letter asking for a balance group contract offer, stating your EIC, the purpose of the balance group (trading, end-customer supply, renewables and so on), the date you want it to start, and any allocation of imbalance to a settlement balance group.

5 · File the mandatory annexes

Anlage 2 — contact data of the BKV is required before the contract can be created. Anlage 1.1 — declaration values tells the TSO how much energy and power you expect to move, and it is what the collateral calculation is based on. Anlage 6 names the traders and suppliers assigned to your group.

6 · Post collateral if the TSO asks

The TSO may require security in justified cases, with written reasons, due within 10 working days. You choose the form: a self-debtor guarantee, a bank guarantee from an EU credit institution, or a pledged account. The TSOs publish a bank-guarantee template.

7 · Connect your schedule management and go live

Set up EDI@Energy messaging, test schedule submission with the TSO, and from the agreed date you are nominating by 14:30 every day and settling monthly.

Check the version of the contract before you rely on a clause number. The Ziffer and Anlage numbers used throughout this page come from the standard contract approved by BNetzA decision BK6-18-061. The TSOs have since published a revised standard contract (Amprion lists one valid from 1 October 2024), and the numbering or wording of individual clauses may have moved. The substance described here is stable; the citations are a starting point, not a final reference.
And the honest recommendation, unchanged. For ten trucks — roughly 6 MWh a day — none of this is worth doing. The collateral formula, a schedule desk reachable every working day and the duty of care in Ziffer 5.2 do not scale down to a single depot. Rent the role (section 7), ideally with your own Unterbilanzkreis so your performance is at least measurable. The reason to understand the requirements is to know what you are paying someone else to carry.

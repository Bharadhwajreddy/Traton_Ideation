Section 1 · start here

What the grid actually needs, and who sells it

Before any of the market machinery makes sense, it helps to know what problem it is all solving. There is only one, and it is brutally simple.

At every instant, the amount of electricity going into the grid must equal the amount coming out. Not on average over the day. Not roughly. At every instant.

Electricity is not stored in the wires. If consumption exceeds generation, the shortfall is taken out of the rotating mass of every generator on the system, they slow down, and the grid frequency falls below 50 Hz. If generation exceeds consumption, frequency rises. Drift far enough — roughly ±0.2 Hz before things get serious, ±2.5 Hz before equipment starts disconnecting itself — and the system begins to come apart.

Two very different jobs follow from that one sentence, and almost all the confusion in this area comes from mixing them up.

Job 1

Get the plan roughly right, in advance

Thousands of buyers and sellers agree in advance who will produce what and who will consume what, slot by slot. By the time delivery arrives, the plan already balances on paper.

This is the energy market — the day-ahead auction and the intraday market. It is a normal commodity market: you buy a thing, you pay for the thing, in €/MWh.

Job 2

Fix whatever the plan got wrong, in real time

No plan is ever right. Wind forecasts miss. A power station trips. A truck comes back from its shift 40 minutes late. Somebody has to hold equipment on standby, under contract, ready to move within seconds.

This is where ancillary services live. And it is a different kind of purchase: you are not buying energy, you are buying capability.

So what exactly is an “ancillary service”?

An ancillary service is anything the system operator needs in order to run the grid that is not the selling of energy itself. The EU definition (Directive 2019/944, Art. 2(48)) reads, in full: “a service necessary for the operation of a transmission or distribution system, including balancing and non-frequency ancillary services, but not including congestion management”.

Read the last five words twice. Congestion management is deliberately carved out of the legal definition. It is still something the TSO has to organise, and it still reaches you as a risk — so it is in the table below as number 5 — but it is not, in law, an ancillary service. Everything else in that table is.

an analogy

An airline sells you a seat. That is its product, and it is what appears on your ticket. But it cannot fly without air-traffic control, de-icing crews, a standby aircraft at the hub, ground power units and a fire service on the runway. Nobody buys a ticket for de-icing. It still has to exist, someone still has to be under contract to provide it, and someone still has to pay for it.

Energy is the seat. Ancillary services are everything else that makes the flight possible.

Now the actual question: is the balancing market the same as the ancillary services market?

This question gets asked constantly and the honest answer has three parts. The short version is at the bottom if you want to skip ahead.

a · Balancing is inside ancillary services, not beside them

They are not two categories to compare. Balancing is family 1 of the five above. Every balancing product (FCR, aFRR, mFRR) is an ancillary service. Most ancillary services are not balancing.

So the relationship is containment, not rivalry — the same relationship as “cardiology” to “medicine”. Asking how they differ is asking the wrong shape of question.

b · There is no such thing as “the ancillary services market”

This is the part that actually resolves the confusion. “Ancillary services” is a category, not a marketplace. Look down the fourth column of the table above: one family is auctioned, one is a compliance obligation, one is negotiated bilaterally, one is not bought at all, and one is a legal instruction you cannot refuse.

There is no single venue, no single price, no single contract, and no single set of participants. Balancing is the only family of the five that is organised as a competitive market in Germany.

put another way

Asking “how does the ancillary services market differ from the balancing market?” is like asking how the transport market differs from the taxi market. The taxi market is a real, definable thing with prices and drivers you can hail. “The transport market” is an umbrella containing taxis, buses, freight rail and bicycles — organised in completely different ways, with different regulators and different money. You cannot buy a ticket for “transport”.

c · Two things make balancing unusual within the family

This is the “is there anything additional in balancing?” part of your question. Yes — two things, and neither has any equivalent in the other four families.

  1. It is split into two separate markets for the same physical thing. You are paid once in €/MW for standing ready (balancing capacity), and again in €/MWh if you are actually called (balancing energy). Two auctions, two prices, two merit orders, one asset. Nothing else in ancillary services works that way — a black-start contract is a single retainer, and reactive power is simply a condition of being connected.
  2. It has a financial twin that applies to everybody. Balancing is the only family that produces an imbalance price — the reBAP — which is charged to or paid to every single market participant, whether or not they have ever sold an ancillary service in their life. Nobody is ever “exposed to the black-start price”. Everybody, always, is exposed to the imbalance price.

What this means if someone offers you “ancillary services revenue”

In Germany, for a truck depot, the only genuinely sellable ancillary services are the three balancing products — FCR, aFRR and mFRR. Voltage support and black start are not open to you. Inertia is not bought. Redispatch is something that happens to you. So when a vendor says “ancillary services revenue”, the correct follow-up question is: which product, which direction, and which of the three payments are you quoting? If they cannot answer that in one sentence, the number is not real.

The three sentences worth memorising

  1. Ancillary services = everything the TSO needs that is not the selling of energy. It is a category, not a marketplace.
  2. Balancing = the frequency slice of that category. It is the one slice that really is a competitive market — in fact two markets (capacity and energy), plus a settlement process (imbalance) that catches everyone.
  3. Most of the rest of the category is not a market at all — it is obligation, bilateral contract, or a legal instruction you cannot refuse.

Section 3

The three payments — and why there have to be three

The same megawatt-hour can be paid for three times, under three contracts, with two different counterparties. That sounds like a swindle until you see what each payment is actually for. Then it becomes obvious.

the intuition, in one picture

Think of a town that contracts a private fire brigade.

  • The retainer. The town pays a fixed monthly fee just to have the crew sitting in the station, trained, with the engine fuelled. They are paid this whether or not there is a fire. That is balancing capacity.
  • The callout. When there is actually a fire, the brigade is paid again — per hour on site, for the water and the crew. Only if they are called. That is balancing energy.
  • The insurance excess. Completely separately, if your building was the one that caught fire because you left a heater on, you pay toward the damage — regardless of whether you have any relationship with the fire brigade at all. That is imbalance settlement.

The first two go to the people with the equipment. The third is charged to whoever caused the problem. They are unrelated contracts that happen to concern the same event.

Why it matters

A pitch quoting “€/MW of aFRR” and a business case quoting “imbalance savings” are describing different contracts with different counterparties. Adding them together without checking is how flexibility business cases get built twice on the same megawatt.

A worked example: one Tuesday at Depot GmbH

Ten electric trucks, a 1.6 MW grid connection. The depot sits inside a service BRP’s balance group and has joined an aggregator’s prequalified aFRR pool. Here is every euro that moves, and who it moves between. Watch how the three payments never touch each other.

Payment 1 · capacity+ €48.00

At 09:00 on Monday the aggregator bids 1 MW of negative aFRR — a promise that the depot can charge harder than planned on command — for the 00:00–04:00 block on Tuesday. The bid clears. The depot has now sold an option.

1 MW × 4 hours × 12 €/MW·h = €48.00

Paid by the TSO to the BSP (the aggregator), who passes on its share. Paid whether or not the depot is ever called. If Tuesday night is uneventful, this €48 is the entire revenue — and the depot did nothing but keep chargers free.

Payment 2 · energy+ €5.10 net

At 01:15 Tuesday the grid is over-supplied — too much wind. PICASSO calls the pool for negative aFRR. The depot’s chargers ramp up for half an hour, absorbing 0.3 MWh more than planned.

0.3 MWh absorbed at the negative-direction marginal price of 45 €/MWh → the depot pays €13.50 for that energy.
But it would otherwise have bought those 0.3 MWh on the intraday market at 62 €/MWh = €18.60.
Net benefit = 18.60 − 13.50 = €5.10

This is the bit people get backwards. In the negative direction you are being paid to consume, so the cash flow looks like buying energy cheaply rather than like receiving a cheque. The energy is also neutralised in the balance group, so it does not show up as an imbalance — you were told to do it.

Payment 3 · imbalance− €8.80

Entirely separately: at 16:45 a truck comes back from its shift 40 minutes late with a lower state of charge than forecast. To make its 06:00 departure the next morning it has to start charging immediately. The depot draws 0.08 MWh more in that quarter hour than its schedule said.

The balance group is short by 0.08 MWh · the reBAP that quarter hour is 110 €/MWh · positive price + short group → you pay
0.08 MWh × 110 €/MWh = €8.80

Nobody called you. Nobody bought anything from you. The TSO simply observed that your group promised one thing and did another, and priced the difference at what energy was worth at that instant. This payment would have happened even if the depot had never heard of the balancing market.

Three lines, three contracts, one Tuesday. Payments 1 and 2 arrive through the aggregator acting as BSP. Payment 3 arrives as a line on the BRP’s monthly invoice. They are settled by different processes, on different timescales, under different agreements — and a depot can be exposed to payment 3 while having no relationship at all with payments 1 and 2.

Payment 3 is the one that behaves strangely

Payments 1 and 2 are intuitive: do a thing, get paid. Payment 3 is the one that catches people out, because it can go either way. Whether you pay or get paid depends on two signs — the sign of the price, and the sign of your own deviation.

Why the sign flip is the whole point

A positive reBAP means the system was short and energy was scarce at that instant. If you took extra, you made it worse — you pay. If you took less than promised, you helped — you are paid.

A negative reBAP means the system was over-supplied and the TSO was paying people to consume. Now the logic inverts: taking extra helped, so you are paid; taking less made it worse, so you pay.

The price is not measuring how badly you forecast. It is measuring what your error did to the system. Get that one idea and section 10 — the one-price versus two-price argument — writes itself.

Section 4

The timeline: what happens when, and who is in charge at each moment

One delivery day, from a week out to the invoice. The thing to hold onto is that the markets and the balancing do not happen one after the other — they overlap.

the whole timeline as one analogy

You book a restaurant table.

  • Day-ahead auction — you reserve a table for four at 8 pm, at tonight’s advertised price.
  • Nomination — you confirm the booking with the restaurant. Now they have staffed and bought food for four covers. This is the step that makes it real.
  • Intraday — at 6 pm you call: make it five. They can still adjust, but the kitchen has already shopped, so the fifth cover costs a bit more than the first four did.
  • Delivery — you turn up with six people.
  • Imbalance settlement — the restaurant charges you for the two extra covers at whatever the walk-in rate was that night. And here is the twist: if they were over-booked and turning people away, the extra covers were a problem and you pay a premium. If the place was half empty, your two extras helped — and in the electricity version of this story you would actually be paid for them.

The restaurant never takes over your booking. It fixes the evening, then bills you for the difference between what you promised and what you did. That is exactly the relationship between you and the TSO.

Look at the two green bars on the right. The TSO is activating reserves all day long — and at the same time, the blue bar shows you are still trading. They run in parallel. There is no moment where one hands over to the other.

Three things people say that are not quite right

“You can’t trade after H-1.”
Inside Germany you can trade until the quarter hour minus 5 minutes. Liquidity thins and the spread widens, but the market is open.
“The TSO starts balancing at H-1.”
It bought the capacity at 08:00–10:00 the previous morning, and FCR has been acting every second since midnight. Balancing never starts, because it never stops.
“The TSO takes over your position.”
It never assumes your obligation. It is the balancer of last resort, not the counterparty of last resort. It fixes the system, then bills your BRP for your share of the error.

Why the TSO’s job is smaller than it sounds

At any moment, hundreds of balance groups are out of position — some short, some long. Before the TSO does anything, those errors cancel against each other. A supplier whose customers used less than forecast offsets a wind farm that produced less than forecast.

The TSO only ever has to cover the net residual. This is why the total volume settled as imbalance energy is routinely far larger than the balancing energy actually activated — and why “how much balancing energy did Germany use” and “how much imbalance was there” are two very different questions with two very different answers.

Section 6

What “contractual aggregation” means

The phrase appears in every European flexibility document and is almost never explained. It is a simple idea about whose permission you need.

Your depot alone is too small to sell flexibility to a TSO. An aggregator signs up many sites, pools them and sells the combination — and to do that it needs to change what your chargers do, remotely, without asking you each time. That is where the problem starts.

Your electricity is already somebody else’s business.

You buy from a supplier, who sits in a BRP’s balance group, and that BRP has already promised the TSO exactly what your meter will draw tomorrow. If a third party makes your trucks charge harder at 01:15, your supplier has to buy energy it never planned to sell you, and your BRP’s balance group goes short and pays the imbalance price. Neither agreed to it; neither is paid for it.

the analogy

You drive a company car with a fuel card your employer pays for. A delivery firm offers you money to run evening errands — but the fuel comes off your employer’s card.

  • Contractual aggregation — the delivery firm must get your employer’s written agreement first. If your employer says no, the deal is dead.
  • Independent aggregation — the law lets the firm hire you anyway, and a central process automatically bills it for the fuel and credits your employer.

That is the whole distinction.

Germany does the first one

It is not a vague norm but a hard prequalification requirement: before a unit can enter the balancing market, the applicant must file a certificate of knowledge and consent (Kenntnis- und Einverständniserklärung) from every market role involved that is not the same legal person.

Whose signatureWhy they get a veto
The connecting grid operatorThe flexibility changes flows on their network

The operator of the asset (you)It is your equipment and your operations at risk
The practical consequence. Your supplier can simply refuse to sign, and refusing costs them nothing. That is why most German flexibility offers arrive as a bundle: the aggregator is also the supplier and the BRP, so all the signatures are their own. If you look at an aggregator that is not your supplier, the first question is not “what is the revenue share?” but “has my supplier agreed to sign?”
Why the signature matters mechanically. Because the BRP consented, the TSO knows an activated volume was its own instruction, and neutralises it in the balance group — the schedule is settled as if it had always included that energy, so the BRP pays nothing and the aggregator is paid. No signature, no neutralisation, and the whole thing collapses into someone else’s imbalance bill.

Independent aggregation — where Europe is heading

Article 17 of Directive (EU) 2019/944 requires Member States to let an aggregator act without the supplier’s or BRP’s agreement — otherwise your supplier can veto every rival and there is no flexibility market, only flexibility bundled into supply contracts. Two mechanisms replace the signature: an automatic imbalance adjustment that removes the delivered volume from the host BRP’s position, and a centrally calculated compensation payment between aggregator and supplier.

Status. Not implemented in German balancing. The TSOs expect the EU Network Code on Demand Response to force it — ACER submitted it to the Commission on 7 March 2025, and it had still not been adopted when this page was last checked in September 2026. The Nordics are building it through eSett. Re-check before citing; this is a genuinely open question.

The one-line version. Contractual aggregation = you need everyone’s permission first. Independent aggregation = the law gives you access and pays them off automatically. Germany is the first; until the second lands, your supplier holds a veto over who may monetise your chargers.

Section 7

“We’ll be your BRP” — what is actually being sold

And the question everybody asks: do they just forward me the invoice, or do they ever pay?

first, the basic

A Bilanzkreis (balance group) is a bank account for electricity. Everything fed in is a deposit; everything consumed or sold on is a withdrawal. The rule is that the account must balance to exactly zero in every 15-minute period — not on average, every period. The BRP is the account holder, and if the account does not balance, the TSO squares it and charges a price set after the fact: the reBAP.

Under § 4 StromNZV every meter in Germany must sit in some balance group at all times — your depot is in one right now, almost certainly your supplier’s. There is no unassigned electricity. What you are buying below is somebody else’s account, and somebody else’s obligation to keep it flat.

first, one word of jargon

“The tail” means the rare, extreme, expensive day. It comes from the shape of a probability chart: most outcomes bunch up in the middle, and the thin stretched-out ends — the tails — are the rare ones. In this context the tail is the quarter hour where the imbalance price goes berserk.

Most days your deviation costs you a few euros. But in a genuine scarcity quarter hour the price can reach 19 998 €/MWh — so being short by a quarter of a megawatt-hour for fifteen minutes costs about €5 000. That single quarter hour is the tail.

So “who carries the tail?” simply means: on that one terrible day, whose money is it? It is the same question as car insurance. Nothing happens in most months; the tail is the crash. You can choose to carry it yourself and pay nothing in the meantime, or pay a premium every month so that somebody else carries it.

Section 8

Buying the power: what happens in the auction

The question asked almost word for word every time: “we don’t know the prices before we bid — so what exactly are we doing?”

You do not submit a price. You submit how much you want at every possible price.

A day-ahead bid is not “1.6 MW at 70 €/MWh”. It is a curve: “for 02:00–02:15 I will take 1.6 MW at or below 70; 0.8 MW between 70 and 120; nothing above 120.” You declare your willingness to pay, not a guess at the outcome.

Every buyer submits a curve like that and every seller the mirror image. The exchange collects them all in secret — this is why it is a blind auction, nobody ever sees anyone else’s bids — stacks them into one demand curve and one supply curve, and finds where they cross.

Uniform pricing

Everyone who clears pays the same price — where the curves cross, not what they bid. So a high bid limit buys certainty, never expense.

It is blind

You submit by 12:00, the algorithm clears all coupled European zones at once, and results appear early in the afternoon. No haggling, no second round.

96 separate clearings

Since 1 October 2025 the German auction clears one product per quarter hour, each with its own price. Your bid curve is per quarter hour too.

So what do you actually submit?

Option 1 · price-dependent

“Charge in this quarter hour only if it is cheap enough.” This is what flexible demand is supposed to do — your bid tells the market what your flexibility is worth.

The catch: if the price comes in above your limit you get nothing that quarter hour. Do that too often and your trucks do not charge.

Option 2 · price-taker, against a forecast

“Forecast tomorrow’s prices, pick the cheapest windows, then bid to clear for certain.” You are guaranteed the energy; you are not guaranteed to have picked the right hours.

This is what almost everyone does. The skill is not in the bidding, it is in the forecast.

How the simulator simplifies this. “Cheapest hours” optimises against the day’s actual cleared prices — a perfect forecast. Real life is option 2 with a worse one, so treat the simulator’s arbitrage saving as an upper bound, not an estimate.

And what is the BRP doing?

They decide — full supply

The BRP forecasts you from history, buys what it thinks you need, nominates it and bills you at your agreed tariff. You are not involved in the auction at all and may never see a day-ahead price — and there is nothing for flexible charging to capture.

You decide — they execute the simulator’s model

You send a charging plan by their cut-off (often 13:00). They place the order on the exchange in your name — you are not an exchange member — and nominate the matching schedule to the TSO by 14:30. The decision is yours; the access is theirs.

What the BRP always does, either way: turn the trade into a nomination. A trade is a contract with a counterparty; a nomination is a promise to the system operator. Until 14:30 D-1 your purchase is paperwork between traders — after it, the TSO has built its reserve plan around your quarter hours.

Intraday is not another auction

Continuous intraday works like a share market: a live order book where a trade happens the instant two offers overlap. So there is a bid–ask spread — you buy at the ask and sell at the bid, and every round trip costs you the gap before any fee. Liquidity thins as delivery approaches, so the spread widens exactly when you most need to trade. You can deal until 5 minutes before the quarter hour inside Germany.

Section 9

The reBAP — the price of getting it wrong

reBAP stands for regelzonenübergreifender einheitlicher Bilanzausgleichsenergiepreis — literally “the single balancing-energy price that applies across all control zones”. One number, per quarter hour, for the whole of Germany.

What is actually being priced here

Rewind fifteen minutes. In that quarter hour, hundreds of balance groups across Germany were slightly wrong: some drew more electricity than they had promised, some drew less. Added together, Germany as a whole ended up either short (consumed more than it generated) or long (generated more than it consumed).

The grid cannot tolerate that, so the TSO fixed it in real time — it telephoned, or rather signalled, the power stations and batteries that were on standby and told them to produce more, or to produce less. That costs money, and somebody has to pay for it. The reBAP is how that cost is turned into a price and charged to the people who caused it.

The three modules, in plain words

German regulation calculates three candidate prices for every quarter hour, then picks one. Each candidate answers a different question.

Module 1 · “what did it actually cost?”

The real, measured price the TSO paid the plants it called. If it needed more power, this is what the last and most expensive megawatt-hour of it cost. This is the honest answer, and most of the time it is the one that wins.

Module 2 · “don’t let anyone game it”

Sometimes fixing the imbalance was cheap — so if the price were left at Module 1, deliberately going short would be cheaper than buying your energy properly on the market. Everyone would do it, and the system would fall apart.

So Module 2 takes the market price at that moment and adds a margin on top. It is a floor when the system is short and a ceiling when it is long, and its only job is to make sure that being imbalanced is never the cheap option.

Module 3 · “we are running out”

Does nothing at all until the imbalance is so big that Germany has used up 80 % of everything it holds in reserve. Past that point the price climbs steeply — not because the last megawatt-hour cost that much, but as a deliberate alarm signal. It is meant to be rare, and on most days it never fires.

Then the rule that picks one of the three

If Germany was short → take the highest of the three, so being short is always painful.
If Germany was long → take the lowest of the three, so being long is never rewarding.

Why single pricing won

  1. Direction, not magnitude. Two-price penalises deviation as such; one-price prices its effect on the system.
  2. Passive balancing is free reserve. Every MW BRPs supply by leaning the right way is a MW the TSO does not activate — and, through dimensioning, eventually a MW it does not procure.
  3. The imbalance price should equal the real-time value of energy. Two-price deliberately breaks that for half the market in every period.
  4. The revenue is a transfer. Under financial neutrality it is recycled anyway, so two-price functions as a tax on forecast error — falling hardest on variable renewables and small portfolios.
  5. It distorts intraday. Facing a spread against the imbalance price, a BRP trades at prices it knows to be bad purely to avoid it. Noise, not price discovery.
  6. It is the EU legal default. EB GL Art. 55 sets single pricing as the model; dual requires explicit approval.

The honest objection — and the real fix

Single pricing does create a speculative incentive to under- or over-nominate if you can forecast the system’s direction. The textbook remedy — cap the imbalance price back at the day-ahead price — is two-price, and throws out the cooperation incentive with the speculation incentive.

What real systems do instead:

  • Module 2. Forces a minimum spread against the intraday index, scaled by the system imbalance. Imbalance is then never cheaper than trading flat, in either direction, at all times.
  • Module 3. In tight periods the price escalates far beyond anything a speculator would risk.
  • Ziffer 5.2. Bilanzkreistreue is a contractual duty. Deliberate systematic imbalance is investigable under Ziffer 11.4, with REMIT on top.
  • 15-minute periods. They shrink both the structural imbalance and the window in which anyone forecasts the system better than the market.
The clean formulation. Single pricing with incentivising and scarcity components dominates dual pricing, because it preserves the property people want from dual pricing — imbalance is never cheaper than trading — without destroying the cooperation signal.

Two German rules about network charges — and which one is yours

This trips up almost every EV-charging business case, so it is worth five slow paragraphs.

§ 14a EnWG — not yours

This is the rule everyone quotes. It says: if you install a big controllable device — a home wallbox, a heat pump, a battery — the grid operator may briefly throttle it at moments of local stress, and in exchange you get a discount on your network charges.

The statute itself says none of this in numbers. The thresholds come from the Bundesnetzagentur decision that implements it, BK6-22-300 of 27 November 2023. Under that decision it applies only if all three are true:

  • the device draws more than 4.2 kW, and
  • it is connected in low voltage — the 400 V wiring a house or small shop is on, and
  • it was commissioned from 1 January 2024 onwards.

A depot pulling 1.6 MW is not on low voltage. It sits on a medium-voltage connection with its own transformer and a meter that records your load every 15 minutes (RLM). So § 14a simply does not reach you. If a vendor's business case leans on it, that number is not real.

§ 19(2) StromNEV — this one is yours

The logic here is different, and rather elegant. Network charges exist to pay for a grid that has to be built big enough for the worst moment of the year — the evening in February when everyone draws at once.

Every network operator publishes the hours in which that worst moment historically falls. They are called Hochlastzeitfenster — “high-load time windows”. Typically a few hours on winter weekday evenings; the exact windows are your operator’s, and published.

The deal: if your own yearly peak reliably lands outside those windows, you are not one of the reasons the grid had to be built that big — so you can be charged individually instead of on the standard tariff. The reduction can be large; the rule allows an individual charge down to as little as 20 % of the standard rate.

Two conditions, and both are hard:

  • It is an agreement with your network operator, not a permit you apply for. There is no ex-ante application deadline. What the 30 September date really is: under BNetzA decision BK4-13-739 the network operator must notify the regulator of the individual charges it has agreed — a reporting duty that runs during the year the charge applies, not a gate you have to pass before it.
  • You must actually stay out of the windows — which means your charging controller has to treat them as a hard no every single day, not a preference it drops when a truck is late.
Why this matters more than anything else on this page. A depot is the near-perfect candidate: its trucks sit still overnight, and overnight is exactly when the high-load windows are not. Moving your peak out of those windows is a software decision, not an investment — and it can beat every energy-trading strategy in this document put together.
And ignore the other branch of § 19(2). Sentence 2, intensive Netznutzung, is far beyond a truck depot. It needs annual consumption exceeding 10 GWh and a tiered number of full-load hours: 7 000 h → 20 %, 7 500 h → 15 %, 8 000 h → 10 % of the published charge. Ten trucks reach neither number.

Section 12

Becoming a BRP yourself: the rules, and who is allowed

Everything above assumed you rent the role. This section is the other answer: what the law actually requires if you want to hold it yourself, and who is eligible.

There is no licence. Being a BRP is a contract, not a permission.

Germany does not issue “BRP authorisations”. You become a Bilanzkreisverantwortlicher by signing a Bilanzkreisvertrag (balance group contract) with a transmission system operator — and under § 26 StromNZV the TSO and the applicant are obliged to conclude that contract, on the standard terms the regulator has approved. The TSO cannot pick and choose.

Who is eligible

Anyone who can meet the contract’s conditions. A BRP is a purely financial role — it owns no power stations and no cables — so there is no technical qualification and no energy-sector licence involved. The actual steps

These are the steps the German TSOs publish. Details differ slightly between the four, so check the page of the TSO whose control area you sit in — the example below follows Amprion’s published process.

1 · Decide which control areas you need

A balance group exists inside one control area. Germany has four TSOs — 50Hertz, Amprion, TenneT DE and TransnetBW — so a nationwide BRP needs at least one balance group, and one contract, in each. A single depot only needs the one its meter sits in.

2 · Get an EIC code

The Energy Identification Code is the unique 16-character identifier every balance group trades under. In Germany it is requested from and issued by the BDEW. Nothing can be registered without it.

3 · Register with the TSO as market role “BKV”

Each TSO runs a portal for market participants — Amprion’s is the Netzwirtschaftsportal. You register there under the market role BKV.

4 · Request a contract offer, in writing

A formal letter asking for a balance group contract offer, stating your EIC, the purpose of the balance group (trading, end-customer supply, renewables and so on), the date you want it to start, and any allocation of imbalance to a settlement balance group.

5 · File the mandatory annexes

Anlage 2 — contact data of the BKV is required before the contract can be created. Anlage 1.1 — declaration values tells the TSO how much energy and power you expect to move, and it is what the collateral calculation is based on. Anlage 6 names the traders and suppliers assigned to your group.

6 · Post collateral if the TSO asks

The TSO may require security in justified cases, with written reasons, due within 10 working days. You choose the form, and there are exactly three: a self-debtor bank guarantee from a credit institution established in the EU, a self-debtor guarantee from an insurance company, or a self-debtor company guarantee capped at 10 % of the guarantor’s liable equity. A pledged bank account is not on the list. The TSOs publish a guarantee template.

7 · Connect your schedule management and go live

Set up EDI@Energy messaging, test schedule submission with the TSO, and from the agreed date you are nominating by 14:30 every day and settling monthly.

Section 13

Limited network access — a story in three countries

Everything before this section was about buying electricity. This section is about something else entirely: the right to draw it at all. It is told as a story, because the rules only make sense once you have watched somebody run into them.

There are two different things you buy in electricity, and almost everyone confuses them.

One is energy — kilowatt-hours, bought day-ahead and intraday. That was sections 1 to 12. The other is capacity — the right to pull a certain number of kilowatts through your connection, at any moment you like. You buy that once, from your grid operator, and then you own it for decades. This section is entirely about the second one.

Prologue: what “firm” means, and why it is expensive

Your depot has a 1.6 MW connection. Today that connection is firm. Firm is a promise the grid operator makes to you: you may draw the full 1.6 MW at any second of any day, forever, and we will have built enough network to let you.

That promise is what you are really paying for in your network charges — and it is why, in a busy area, a new connection can take years. The operator cannot promise what the cables cannot carry.

hold on to this picture

A firm connection is a reserved parking space with your name painted on it. Always free, always yours, and you pay for the certainty that nobody else can ever take it.

Limited network access is a season ticket to the car park. Much cheaper, available now — and on the handful of days when the car park is genuinely full, you are turned away.

The whole of this section is two questions: how often is it full, and who decides?

Part one · Germany

Anna and the substation that is already full

Anna runs a haulage depot outside Leipzig. Ten electric trucks are on order. She writes to her distribution grid operator — her DSO — and asks for a 1.6 MW connection.

She is on reasonable ground. § 17 Abs. 1 EnWG obliges grid operators to connect customers on terms that are „angemessen, diskriminierungsfrei, transparent“ — reasonable, non-discriminatory, transparent. And the list of things they must connect names, word for word, „Ladepunkte für Elektromobile“. Charging points for electric vehicles are written into the connection obligation. Anna is not asking for a favour.

But be precise about what that obligation is, because this is where people overstate the law. § 17 Abs. 1 gives Anna a right to request a connection and to be treated reasonably, transparently and without discrimination. It does not give her an unconditional right to 1.6 MW, immediately, at the connection point she picked. The 1.6 MW is her requested capacity, not an entitlement — and § 17 Abs. 2, below, is the operator’s way out.
the reply she gets

“We cannot give you 1.6 MW. The substation feeding your road is already committed — two battery projects and a logistics park applied before you. To connect you firmly we would have to reinforce. That is a queue of several years and a construction cost contribution of several hundred thousand euros.”

Anna is now stuck between two useless answers: full power, in several years, or nothing. Her trucks arrive in eight months.

But she has one right worth using. Under § 17 Abs. 2 EnWG the operator may refuse — only if it proves connection is impossible or unreasonable, only in writing with reasons, and, if Anna asks, the reasons must include „aussagekräftige Informationen“ about which reinforcement works would be needed and what they would cost. That entitlement is hers, and it is the lever for everything that follows.

The third answer

There is a middle path between “yes, in five years” and “no”:

the offer that unblocks her

“Connect now. Use your full 1.6 MW almost all the time. But we keep the right to hold you below it when this substation is under strain — and you accept that in writing.”

That is a flexible Netzanschlussvereinbarung — in English a flexible connection agreement, FCA. In Germany it has existed since February 2025, under § 17 Abs. 2b EnWG.

So can Anna simply ask for one?

This is the question everybody asks, and the Bundesnetzagentur answers it directly in its own FAQ. Read these three sentences slowly, because between them they contain the whole answer.

„FCA dienen dazu, bei knappen Netzanschlusskapazitäten Netzanschlüsse in möglichst hohem Umfang mit Einschränkungen zu ermöglichen.“

“Where connection capacity is scarce.” This is not a product you shop for. The situation in which an FCA becomes relevant is normally the one where the firm capacity you asked for cannot be provided — not simply that you would prefer a flexible arrangement. Put carefully: the regulator ties FCAs to scarce capacity, and nothing in the law creates a right to demand one because you like the idea.

„Es gibt derzeit die Möglichkeit, aber keine explizite gesetzliche Verpflichtung für Netzbetreiber, FCA-Anschlüsse anzubieten.“

No obligation to offer. Anna has no right to an FCA. She cannot demand one, and no court will hand her one.

„Wenn der Netzbetreiber den Anschluss im gewünschten Leistungsumfang nicht bereitstellen kann, sollte er prüfen, ob der Netzanschluss alternativ durch einen FCA-Vertrag ermöglicht werden kann. Eine begründungslose Verweigerung oder eine Verweigerung allein aus generellen, ablehnenden Erwägungen entspricht nicht dem Ziel des Gesetzes.“

And there it is. A refusal with no reasons, or a blanket “we don’t do those”, is contrary to the purpose of the law — in the regulator’s own words.

So the answer is neither “yes, on request” nor “only if they feel like it”. It is a third thing.

The DSO decides whether to offer; Anna decides whether to accept. Neither can force the other. But once the DSO has said it cannot give her firm capacity, it should examine an FCA, and it cannot refuse in a single blank sentence.

Which means Anna’s move is not “give me an FCA”. It is: “You have told me in writing that you cannot provide 1.6 MW. The Bundesnetzagentur says you should now examine whether an FCA could work here. Please do, and put it in writing if you won’t.” That is an email she can send this afternoon.

Which shape does she get?

Anna does not pick from a menu the way a Dutch customer does. The shape follows the grid problem, and the DSO’s engineers are the ones who diagnose what operating envelope the network can actually tolerate. But it is still an agreement. Anna does not choose the product unilaterally — and equally she is not obliged to accept whatever terms are put in front of her. The details are contractually agreed between the two of them.

The statute names only two shapes:

„statisch“ — one fixed ceiling

A single number, always in force. You may never exceed 1.0 MW.

For Anna: the easy one. She designs the charging plan around it once and never thinks about it again.

„dynamisch“ — a ceiling in windows

Lower in defined, known-in-advance periods. For example — and this is an illustration, not a German standard — not above 0.8 MW on winter weekdays, 17:00–20:00. The regulator gives “certain hours around midday” as its own example. There is no national FCA schedule; the windows are whatever your contract says.

For Anna: fine, and possibly free. Her trucks are out driving at 18:00. She may be giving up almost nothing.

Do not confuse the shape of the limit with the life of the agreement. These are two separate dials, and mixing them up is a common error. Shape is static (a fixed ceiling) or dynamic (a ceiling that varies by time). Duration is befristet — temporary, until the network is reinforced — or dauerhaft, permanent. The regulator confirms both durations are possible. So “static” does not mean “permanently smaller connection”. You can have a static limit on a temporary agreement, or a dynamic limit that lasts forever. Four combinations, and the contract has to say which one you have signed.
You will read about a third shape. Treat it carefully. Law-firm briefings and operator material describe a „volldynamisch“ option — fully dynamic, where the operator cuts you at short notice based on live grid conditions. That is real commentary, but it is not in the statute, and the regulator treats it as not yet ready: using fully dynamic concepts „setzt voraus, dass vorher geeignete, netzebenübergreifende Prozesse entwickelt und vorgehalten werden“ — it requires cross-grid-level data processes that largely do not exist yet. MITNETZ STROM says the same: it is their goal, not their product. In practice today you will be offered static or windowed-dynamic.
One line that connects this section straight to the simulator. The regulator says FCA limits must be applied „zeitlich vor dem Day-Ahead-Handel“ — before day-ahead trading — so that questions of balancing never arise. In the language of section 4: the limit has to be known before you bid. Otherwise you promise power you are not allowed to take, and the difference becomes imbalance.

What does it actually save her? Less than you would think

The usual selling point is the Baukostenzuschuss — the one-off construction cost contribution, calculated from the capacity you book. Book less firm capacity, pay less. Vendors quote reductions of up to 80 %.

Be careful here. A legal analysis from the Stiftung Umweltenergierecht — an independent research foundation, not a vendor — puts it much more cautiously, and raises a point that changes the business case:

It is legally open whether the BKZ can be cut at all

Whether an FCA may be paired with a reduced construction cost contribution was „vom Gesetzgeber offengelassen“ — left open by the legislator. There is a good argument for it (the FCA adapts the connection to what the grid actually needs), the amount must be angemessen under § 17 Abs. 1, and the operator must apply a general, non-discriminatory method. But it is not a right.

And it may only be deferred, not waived

If the network is later reinforced and Anna gets a „vollständiger und dauerhafter“ connection, the analysis warns of „ggf. Anfall BKZ in voller Höhe“ — the full contribution may then fall due.

So the headline saving may be a deferral, not a discount. Anna must ask, in writing, what happens to the BKZ on the day her connection becomes firm.

So do not build a business case on the BKZ saving. On the independent legal reading it is not bankable — it is an open question with a possible clawback attached. The reliable benefits of an FCA are speed and getting connected at all. Treat any quoted BKZ saving as a negotiating position, and get the reinforcement scenario in writing.

“Then is there any benefit at all?”

This is the right question to ask after reading the paragraph above, and the answer is yes — but it is a different kind of benefit from the one the brochures promise. Three things need separating.

You do not pay twice

The Baukostenzuschuss is a one-off payment for the capacity you book (bestellte Leistung × the published Leistungspreis). If you later book more capacity, a contribution is charged on the increase.

So the warning is about a clawback of a discount, not about paying for the same kilowatts a second time. That is a real risk, but it is a much smaller one than “pay it all again”.

The comparison is not what you think

The alternative to an FCA is not “the same connection, firm, at the same price”. It is waiting years, or not connecting.

So the benefit is the value of operating in those years — for ten trucks, that is the whole business case — plus the money you did not tie up early. Paying later is worth more than paying now, even at the same nominal figure.

You may never convert at all

The clawback only arrives if you take a firm connection later. An FCA may be dauerhaft — permanent — and the regulator says so explicitly.

If a limit bites at 17:00–20:00 and your trucks are out driving then, you may simply never need to upgrade. Converting is an option you hold, not an obligation you carry.

So the honest verdict on the German economics: the benefit is real, but it is timing and optionality, not a subsidy — and it is unpriced.

You get connected years earlier, you avoid financing a reinforcement you might never need, and you keep the choice of going firm later. What you do not get in Germany is a published discount, a guaranteed reduction, or a written rule for what happens on conversion. All three exist in Denmark, in the document quoted below.

That is the thesis argument, and it is a stronger one than “FCAs are good”: the instrument is economically sound and legally available, and Germany has left the one thing that makes it bankable — the price — entirely to a bilateral negotiation in which the depot is the smaller party.

And if the DSO simply says no?

Anna’s lawyer suggests a Missbrauchsantrag — a formal abuse complaint to the regulator. The regulator, in the same FAQ, talks her out of it:

„Ein Missbrauchsantrag ist ein formales juristisches Verfahren, das erheblich Zeit in Anspruch nimmt… Eine Beschleunigung des Anschlussbegehrens ist dadurch nicht zu erreichen. Auch an der objektiven Knappheit der zur Verfügung stehenden Anschlusskapazität ändert sich dadurch nichts.“

You may complain. It will be slow, it will not connect you faster, and it will not create capacity that does not exist. What the regulator recommends instead is that the industry build standard FCA contracts — and it says it supports such efforts „nachdrücklich“, emphatically.

Hold on to that last sentence. It is exactly what the next two countries have already done — and it is the thread that ties this whole story together.

Marieke, who is handed a menu

Marieke runs a bus depot near Rotterdam. Same problem as Anna: a congested grid and a long queue. What happens next is entirely different.

Her grid operator does not decide whether to offer her a flexible contract. In a congestion area it has to. Optional from 1 February 2024; mandatory from 1 February 2025.

And she is not handed one vague “flexible” product to negotiate. She is handed a menu — approved by the regulator ACM in July 2024, open to large connections (above 3×80 A):

Part three · Denmark

Lars, who gets to choose

Lars runs a distribution depot outside Copenhagen. Denmark calls the idea begrænset netadgang — “limited network access”, the plainest name any of the three countries has given it.

Everything in this part comes from one document: the sector guideline „Vilkår og betingelser for tilslutning med begrænset netadgang for forbrugsanlæg“ — for consumption facilities, not generation — notified to the Danish regulator Forsyningstilsynet under § 73b of the Electricity Supply Act in December 2019, taken to effect 20 March 2020, and amended since, most recently in 2024. The link is in the sources table at the end.

The difference from Anna’s story, in one line.

Anna reaches a flexible connection after being told she cannot have a firm one. Lars can ask for limited access directly — the guideline says both new connections and extensions of an existing supply may request it. It is a product he can choose, not only a rescue after a refusal.

Act 1 — Lars works out what he needs, and finds three doors

Lars needs a maximum grid import of about 2 MW. In Denmark that opens three options, not two:

A · Full access

The ordinary connection, ordinary security of supply, ordinary published connection charge, prices and tariffs.

B · Limited access

The whole 2 MW is interruptible. Cheapest to connect, and the riskiest thing a truck depot could sign.

C · Partly limited

Delvist begrænset netadgang. Part of the site firm, part interruptible. This is the one a depot should want — see the design box at the end of this part.

Act 2 — Is Lars even eligible?

Not every site qualifies. The distribution-level framework is aimed at business customers with interruptible consumption connected at 10–60 kV. A small depot on ordinary low voltage does not automatically fall inside it. And the guideline is explicit that practical connection „afhænger af de lokale forhold og betingelser i det enkelte netselskab“ — it depends on the individual grid company’s local conditions, and that company must have adopted the approved method.

And the guideline will talk you out of it in the wrong location. It states plainly that the arrangement „ikke [er] attraktiv for kunder, som beder om tilslutning til steder i nettet, hvor der ikke er ledig kapacitet“ — not attractive where there is no spare capacity, because you would simply be interrupted very often — and that the grid company must tell you this when you ask. Limited access is for places with capacity in most network states, not a way into a genuinely full network.

Act 3 — What Lars actually stops paying (read this carefully)

This is the single most misread part of the Danish scheme, so here it is precisely.

Reduced: the one-off connection payment

„tilslutningsbetalingen er reduceret ift. betalingen ved tilslutning på de almindelige vilkår“ — the connection payment is reduced against what you would pay on ordinary terms. In the original heat-pump scheme the tilslutningsbidrag was removed outright.

This is capital, not tariff. It is the up-front contribution for building the connection — Denmark’s counterpart to the German Baukostenzuschuss.

Not reduced: everything ongoing

Lars still pays „de løbende priser i form af abonnement og tariffer som alle andre kunder“ — the running subscription and network tariffs like every other customer.

In fact he pays more: an extra subscription for the special operational monitoring the limited access requires, plus every actual cost of the connection bay, control and monitoring gear, online metering, the remote-control interface, the communication channels, and the case handling and design work.

So “you stop paying for network capacity” would be the wrong way to put it. You get a discount on the one-off cost of getting connected. Your monthly network bill is normal, and slightly higher because of the monitoring. The premise of the whole scheme is that the connection happens „uden meromkostninger for nettets øvrige kundegrupper“ — without shifting costs onto everyone else — which is exactly why the ongoing tariffs cannot be discounted.
And the corresponding restriction. On limited access Lars uses only the existing spare capacity. He cannot pay to have the transformer or the upstream network reinforced to get more, without moving to a full connection agreement. The connection must not trigger uncovered reinforcement investment locally or upstream.

Act 4 — How the limit is enforced: a live control link

Here is the deepest difference from Germany. A German FCA is a number in a contract that you keep yourself under. Danish limited access is a control link the grid company operates.

the control chain

1 · Lars builds the link and pays for it. Automatic control-system monitoring and remote control, online metering with remote transfer of meter data to the grid company’s control centre (driftscentral), plus his own local control equipment, meter board, the interface to their remote-control equipment and the communication channels.

2 · The link is fail-safe, and this clause is the striking one. The control system must be built so that „et negativt eller et manglende signal fra fjernkontrollen vil udkoble anlægget og blokere for indkobling“ — a negative or missing signal disconnects the site and blocks reconnection. Silence is not permission.

3 · Turn down rather than trip, where possible. If the load can be modulated, the guideline prefers a control that reduces power over a full disconnection. For a charging depot this is everything — chargers can throttle; a heat pump mostly cannot.

4 · If monitoring and control cannot be implemented — or would be disproportionately complicated — the grid company must refuse the limited-access connection. No control link, no product.

Act 5 — When the grid is short, who gets cut?

There is a genuine trap here, and it is worth getting right because Denmark uses two different rules depending on whether you are consuming or generating.

Consumption — proportional

„alle kunder blive beordret til at reducere deres effekttræk med samme procentvise andel af den samlede tilsluttede effekt med begrænset netadgang“.

Where several limited-access customers sit in the same area and the constraint can be solved by curtailing any of them, all are cut by the same percentage of their connected limited-access capacity. This is the rule that applies to a truck depot.

Production — last in, first out

The parallel guideline for production facilities uses a different principle: the grid company curtails the most recently connected installation first.

Do not mix these up. Commentary written about Danish solar and wind describes the last-in-first-out rule; it is from the production document and does not govern a consumption site.

And the risk is local, not national. Two depots in different parts of Denmark do not face the same interruption frequency — it depends on the constrained substation each sits behind. Sites at the same location see roughly the same risk because they share the same physical grid.

Act 6 — How much warning does Lars get?

What happensNotice
Your limitation is increased — a new large customer connects, an existing one expands, or the network is rebuilt „varsles af netselskabet på forhånd“ — notified in advance. The sector guideline does not fix a period; individual grid companies’ connection agreements may set one. Check yours.
A network fault, and the temporary rebuilds or reconfigurations that follow „kan ikke varsles“ — cannot be notified. No warning.
Abnormal switching states in normal operation You may be cut or turned down, in both situation 1 and situation 2.
This is why you will read both “with notice” and “without notice” about Denmark. Both are true. You are warned when the grid plans to squeeze you and not when it breaks. A depot has to survive the second case on its own.

Act 7 — How many hours will he lose? Nobody will promise

The grid company must give an estimate at connection of how many hours a year the capacity can be expected to be available, based on historical loading data and expected load growth. And then:

„Netselskabet kan på ingen måde gøres ansvarlig for, at dette timetal overholdes, og ejeren af anlægget bærer det fulde økonomiske ansvar for begrænsninger i netadgangen.“

The grid company cannot in any way be held responsible for that number being met, and the owner carries the full economic risk. So Denmark gives you a better forecast than Germany and a weaker promise: the estimate is planning information, not a contractual cap. Unless your individual agreement makes it binding, that is all it is.

And the risk clause reaches into the rest of this document. The owner bears all direct and indirect losses, expressly including „ubalance i afregning“ — imbalance settlement — and loss from being unable to deliver regulating reserves. If the grid company turns Lars down after he has promised that power to his balance group, the imbalance cost is his. Germany attacks the same hazard from the other end, by telling operators to set FCA limits before day-ahead trading. Two countries, one collision, two different fixes.

Act 8 — The contract, and who checks it

In Germany the FCA is private and nobody but the two parties sees it. In Denmark the contract itself is supervised.

RequirementWhat the guideline says
The document A nettilslutningsaftale carrying the specific technical and economic terms, with the special access conditions and cost allocation written into it. It comes with appendices — connection basis, timetable and payments, technical basis, special conditions, documentation, operating notifications.
Regulator sign-off The agreement must be notified to and method-approved by Forsyningstilsynet. A grid company may use the sector standard agreement if it notifies the regulator; a bespoke one must be notified and method-approved too. No unsupervised private contract.
Mandatory honesty The uncertainty about how much access you will have, now and in future, must be made clear to the customer and written into the agreement, together with the fact that the customer bears the full economic loss.
Right-sizing The capacity made available must match your actual current need, and the grid company may demand documentation of that need at any time. You cannot book headroom you do not use.

Act 9 — If Lars later wants firm access

„Såfremt samme tilslutningspunkt kan anvendes, fratrækkes en standardrabat for feltomkostning… Tidligere etableringsomkostninger og løbende omkostninger til automatisk kontrolsystem­overvågning og fjernkontrol tilbageføres ikke til kunden ved overgang til fuld netadgang.“

You pay the ordinary connection contribution at that point, minus a published standard rebate for the bay cost if the same connection point can be reused. What you already spent on establishing the connection and on the control and monitoring equipment is not refunded. Conversion may also need a fresh network assessment, reinforcement and revised technical arrangements.

Do not assume limited access ripens into firm access. Under the ordinary product the grid is not reinforced on account of permanently limited-access customers — only full-access demand drives network dimensioning. At transmission level Denmark separates this cleanly: permanent limited access means you accept long-term interruptibility and do not cause reinforcement, while temporary limited access is for a customer who ultimately wants full access and is counted as future full-access demand in planning. That distinction is the clearest thing Germany could copy — § 17 Abs. 2b allows both durations but draws no economic or planning line between them.
The catch, as of this writing: Denmark has paused it for larger customers. Because of capacity constraints in Energinet’s transmission network — and after the regulator rejected a proposed model — grid companies reported in August 2026 that they cannot enter new limited-access connection agreements for larger commercial customers before autumn 2026 at the earliest, with „endnu ingen sikker tidshorisont for en løsning“ — still no confirmed timeline. Energinet is consulting on a revised combined TSO–DSO arrangement. Separately, Energinet paused new large-load transmission connection agreements on 2 March 2026, with a queue reported near 60 GW against a national peak of about 7 GW, and dropped first-come-first-served for large loads on 1 February 2026. Denmark has the better framework and, right now, no more room than Germany.

The design lesson for a truck depot — and it is not “go fully flexible”.

A fully interruptible depot is a depot that can miss a departure. The right Danish structure is option C: a firm base covering the building, workshop, IT and the chargers serving trucks with urgent departures — plus a limited-access layer carrying the schedulable overnight charging that can genuinely be turned down.

Worked through: 0.5 MW firm + 1.5 MW limited = 2.0 MW normally. In a severe local restriction the limited layer can go to zero and Lars still has 0.5 MW — enough to keep the site alive and the urgent trucks moving. That is a survivable failure mode. A single 2 MW limited-access connection is not.

Same question, three different people holding the pen

All three countries ask the customer for the same thing: give up firmness, get connected sooner. What differs is who writes the deal.

The one sentence to take away.

Germany created the legal instrument but not the market for it. § 17 Abs. 2b exists, it covers withdrawal, and it covers your depot — but there is no product, no price, no menu and no duty to offer. The Netherlands created the instrument and the products. Denmark created the instrument and the standard contract and the tariff consequence — and let consumers have it first.

Germany is not behind on the law. It is behind on the paperwork.

What this means for your depot, honestly

Why it fits you almost suspiciously well

Grid stress in Germany lands on winter weekday evenings. Your trucks are out driving then. They charge overnight, when the network is empty. A windowed-dynamic limit aimed at 17:00–20:00 might cost you nothing at all.

And in the simulator, the largest single line on the depot’s bill is not the electricity — it is the network charge driven by peak power. This is the instrument that attacks that number from the connection side.

And the one risk a battery does not have

Every live product is designed for storage. A battery that cannot charge loses revenue. A truck that cannot charge cannot do the job it exists for.

If a cut lands at 04:00 and a truck leaves at 06:00 under-charged, that is a missed delivery, and no FCA carries that cost for you. This is why the Danish terms — 0 to 100 %, without notice — would be dangerous for a depot even though they are cheap.

The four things to settle before signing anything. The statute requires an FCA to state the level of the limit, the periods, the duration, the technical requirements and your liability for exceeding it. Those are the headings. These are the answers to fight for: (1) a ceiling on curtailment expressed in hours per year; (2) minimum notice before a cut, in writing; (3) what happens to the Baukostenzuschuss on the day the connection becomes firm; (4) whether the agreement restricts balancing-market participation — several German drafts cap aFRR at 30 % of capacity and exclude FCR outright.

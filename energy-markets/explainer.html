Germany · reference

How the German electricity market
actually works

There is a market operator that clears day-ahead and intraday. There is a TSO that does balancing. There is a company offering to “be your BRP”. Those three sentences are all true and none of them tells you how the pieces fit together. This page traces one megawatt-hour from the forward market to the monthly invoice, names every counterparty, and quotes the clause or the formula behind each claim.

Section 1

Four words that get used loosely and mean precise things

Most of the confusion in this area is vocabulary, not economics. Get these four right and the rest follows.

Ancillary services

Everything a TSO buys or mandates to run the system: frequency, voltage, restoration, inertia, congestion.

Directive 2019/944 Art. 2(48), 31, 40 · §§ 12, 13 EnWG

Balancing a subset

Only the part concerned with the active power balance, and therefore with frequency.

Regulation (EU) 2017/2195 “EB GL”

FCR / aFRR / mFRR / RR

The four standard products through which balancing is delivered. Germany does not use RR.

EB GL + SO GL 2017/1485

Imbalance settlement

The ex-post financial process that charges each portfolio for its deviation. No energy moves.

EB GL Art. 44–55 · MaBiS BK6-07-002
Section 2

One megawatt-hour, three payments, two counterparties

This is the single most useful idea on the page. Confusing these three is the number-one source of muddle in every conversation about flexibility revenue.

Payment 1 + 2

Rewards for being useful. You bid, you win, you deliver on command. Capacity pays you to stand ready; energy pays you when the TSO actually calls.

Payment 3

Not a reward and not a penalty — a price. It reprices the gap between what your balance group promised and what it did, in whichever direction that gap points.

The trap

A flexibility pitch that quotes “€/MW of aFRR” and a business case that quotes “imbalance savings” are talking about different contracts with different counterparties. Ask which one.

Section 3

The timeline: where the exchange stops and the TSO starts

The common mental model — “markets until H-1, then the TSO takes over” — is almost right and misleading in three specific ways. Markets and balancing overlap in time. The TSO bought its reserves the previous morning. And it never assumes your obligation: it fixes the system, then bills you for your share of the error.

years → days
before

Forward / OTC market EEX · brokers

Purely financial hedging. Does not create a schedule. A forward position has to be turned into a day-ahead nomination eventually, or it settles financially against the index.

D-1 · 08:00
09:00 · 10:00

Balancing capacity auctions TSO

A completely separate market on regelleistung.net, open only to prequalified BSPs. FCR closes 08:00, aFRR 09:00, mFRR 10:00. Six 4-hour blocks. Minimum bid 1 MW. FCR is symmetric and pay-as-cleared; aFRR and mFRR are directional and pay-as-bid.

D-1 · 12:00

Day-ahead auction EPEX SPOT · Nord Pool

One blind auction under SDAC, results around 12:45. Since 1 October 2025: 96 quarter-hour products. Uniform clearing price per MTU, EUPHEMIA, −500 to +4 000 €/MWh. This is where 80–90 % of volume is priced and it is the reference for everything else.

D-1 · 14:30

Schedule nomination your BRP → TSO

The step everyone skips. Under Anlage 3 Ziffer 1.3 of the Standard-Bilanzkreisvertrag the BRP must transmit quarter-hourly schedules by 14:30 on D-1, and the balance group must be nominated balanced. This is the moment a trade becomes a physical promise. Your BRP will impose its own earlier cut-off on you.

D-1 15:00
→ QH − 5 min

Intraday SIDC / XBID · IDA1–3 auctions

Continuous trading opens 15:00 D-1. Cross-border closes 60 minutes before delivery; inside the German bidding zone you can trade until 5 minutes before. Price range −9 999 to +9 999 €/MWh — a number that reappears literally inside the imbalance price formula. Intraday schedule changes need at least one quarter hour of lead time.

D · QH − 25 min

Balancing energy market (RAM) TSO

15-minute products, positive and negative. Gate closure 25 minutes before the delivery quarter hour. Paid at the cross-border marginal price from PICASSO (aFRR) and MARI (mFRR). Bid cap 15 000 €/MWh. Winning capacity obliges you to bid energy. Since the abolition of Freisetzung on 8 December 2022, opportunity cost is sunk and the rational strategy is to bid at marginal cost.

D · real time

Activation automatic and continuous

FCR acts in ≤30 s, autonomously, proportional to frequency deviation, across the whole synchronous area — no merit order and no energy payment at all. aFRR in ≤5 min via PICASSO, which re-optimises every 4 seconds — up to 225 cycles per quarter hour. mFRR in ≤12.5 min via MARI. IGCC nets opposite demands across TSOs before anything is activated.

D + weeks

Imbalance settlement TSO ↔ BRP ↔ you

Per quarter hour, per balance group: all withdrawals (including schedule-based) versus all injections (including schedule-based). Multiply by the reBAP, net over the billing month, one invoice (Ziffer 11). Due at the earliest two weeks after receipt. Objections about data supplied by the grid operator or metering operator cannot be raised against the TSO.

Not true: “you can’t trade after H-1.” Inside Germany you can trade until the quarter hour minus 5 minutes.
Not true: “the TSO starts balancing at H-1.” Capacity was bought the previous morning and FCR is acting every second of the day.
Not true: “the TSO takes over your position.” It is the balancer of last resort, never the counterparty of last resort. Your BRP is your counterparty.

Contractual aggregation Germany today

Prequalification requires a written certificate of knowledge and consent from the connecting grid operator, the supplier, the BRP and the operator wherever those roles are not the same legal person.

Consequence: an aggregator in Germany cannot activate your depot over the head of your supplier and your BRP. Expect to move your supply contract, or to get your supplier to sign.

Independent aggregation the EU target model

Article 17 of Directive (EU) 2019/944 requires Member States to enable a BSP acting over resources belonging to another BRP, with automatic imbalance adjustment and a compensation payment between the parties.

Not implemented in German balancing. The TSOs expect the EU Network Code on Demand Response to force it — ACER submitted it on 7 March 2025; not adopted as of early 2026. The Nordics are building it through eSett. This is a live regulatory question.

Section 5

“We’ll be your BRP” — what is actually being sold

And the question everybody asks: do they just forward me the invoice, or do they ever pay?

The bundle

  1. Access. Their Bilanzkreis, their EIC, their contract with the TSO — so you need none of those, and no collateral with the TSO.
  2. Nomination. Your schedule by 14:30 D-1 and your intraday changes, in the correct EDI@Energy format, to the right TSO.
  3. Market access. Often exchange membership too, for day-ahead and intraday execution.
  4. Risk absorption. How much — entirely contractual. See the table.
  5. Settlement handling. They receive the TSO’s monthly invoice for the whole balance group and re-allocate it.

The three commercial models that exist

B · Pooled / netted Nets your deviation against other customers in the same Bilanzkreis first, passes on your share of the residual A higher €/MWh fee in exchange Shared. Diversification is the product

So: yes, a BRP can absolutely end up paying you. In model C it pays by construction. In model B it pays whenever your deviation is offset by another customer’s. Only in model A does it purely pass the bill through — and even then it carries your credit risk toward the TSO, which is why it asks for collateral. If a quoted fee looks suspiciously low, you are in model A and you own the tail.

Checklist for reading an offer

☐ Which of A / B / C is it, in writing?

☐ Is imbalance netted inside the Bilanzkreis before allocation, or per customer?

☐ Is there a tolerance band, and what happens outside it?

☐ Is the reBAP passed through symmetrically? A contract that charges you the full price but credits you only part of it is a private two-price scheme.

☐ Who keeps the upside when you are long in a negative-price hour?

☐ What nomination cut-off do they impose on you — often 13:00, not 14:30?

☐ Collateral: how much, in what form, reviewed how often?

☐ Termination notice, and what happens to your metering points afterwards?

Section 6

The reBAP: three modules and a max/min rule

One price per quarter hour for the whole German control block, applying to all balance groups regardless of direction. Move the sliders and watch which module binds.

System state

Positive = the German control block is short.

Dimensioning used here: aFRR 1 922 / 1 842 MW, mFRR 681 / 372 MW, AbLa 750 MW, capacity reserve 1 056 MW.

Module 1 · base

—

Volume-weighted PICASSO (aFRR, re-optimised every 4 s) and MARI (mFRR) marginal prices for the activated direction. If nothing was activated: the Value of Avoided Activation.

Module 2 · incentivising

—

ID AEP ± ΔP where ΔP = max(10·s , |ID|·0.25·s), s = min(125, |Balance|·0.25)/125.

Module 3 · scarcity

—

A parabola anchored on Module 2, from the 80 % dead band up to twice the intraday bid cap where every reserve is exhausted.

reBAP

—

Asymmetric quarter hour. With the capacity reserve activated and the system short beyond all available positive FRR, § 32 KapResV makes short balance groups pay at least 2 × 9 999 = 19 998 €/MWh while long groups settle at the normal price. This is the only case where the German imbalance price differs by direction.

The sign flip is the part that surprises everyone the first time. It is not a quirk — it is the whole design: the price reflects what your deviation did to the system, not how big it was.

Where the money actually lands

Imbalance revenue and balancing cost do not match. The TSOs sum the annual deficit or surplus and roll it into network tariffs (NNE) — which makes them financially neutral and shifts the residual onto network users.

So the textbook “budget balanced” property is approximate, and imbalance charges are a transfer, not cost recovery. That matters for the next section.

Also: imbalance volumes greatly exceed activated balancing energy, because balance groups net against each other before the TSO sees anything.

Section 7

One price or two: the argument, settled

Germany and the Nordics both use single pricing. About five EU TSOs still use dual. Here is why, with the honest objection and what the systems actually do about it.

System stateYour deviationIt isOne-priceTwo-price
shortover-produce / under-consumehelping33 paid30 paid
shortunder-produce / over-consumeaggravating33 charged33 charged
longover-produce / under-consumeaggravating27 paid27 paid
longunder-produce / over-consumehelping27 charged30 charged

Day-ahead 30 €/MWh, imbalance 33 when short and 27 when long. Read the last two columns as incentives. On the aggravating rows the two schemes are identical. They differ only on the helping rows — and there, two-price replaces a reward with the day-ahead price, i.e. with indifference.

Why single pricing won

  1. Direction, not magnitude. Two-price penalises deviation as such; one-price prices its effect on the system.
  2. Passive balancing is free reserve. Every MW BRPs supply by leaning the right way is a MW the TSO does not activate — and, through dimensioning, eventually a MW it does not procure.
  3. The imbalance price should equal the real-time value of energy. Two-price deliberately breaks that for half the market in every period.
  4. The revenue is a transfer. Under financial neutrality it is recycled anyway, so two-price functions as a tax on forecast error — falling hardest on variable renewables and small portfolios.
  5. It distorts intraday. Facing a spread against the imbalance price, a BRP trades at prices it knows to be bad purely to avoid it. Noise, not price discovery.
  6. It is the EU legal default. EB GL Art. 55 sets single pricing as the model; dual requires explicit approval.

The honest objection — and the real fix

Single pricing does create a speculative incentive to under- or over-nominate if you can forecast the system’s direction. The textbook remedy — cap the imbalance price back at the day-ahead price — is two-price, and throws out the cooperation incentive with the speculation incentive.

What real systems do instead:

  • Module 2. Forces a minimum spread against the intraday index, scaled by the system imbalance. Imbalance is then never cheaper than trading flat, in either direction, at all times.
  • Module 3. In tight periods the price escalates far beyond anything a speculator would risk.
  • Ziffer 5.2. Bilanzkreistreue is a contractual duty. Deliberate systematic imbalance is investigable under Ziffer 11.4, with REMIT on top.
  • 15-minute periods. They shrink both the structural imbalance and the window in which anyone forecasts the system better than the market.
The clean formulation. Single pricing with incentivising and scarcity components dominates dual pricing, because it preserves the property people want from dual pricing — imbalance is never cheaper than trading — without destroying the cooperation signal.

Note the ordering. Grid-charge optimisation usually beats every energy-market strategy for a depot, and it needs no market role at all. Anyone pitching V2G revenue that ignores the Leistungspreis is selling you the fourth-best thing first.

Prequalify for the negative direction first. Downward flexibility from a charging depot is close to free. Upward flexibility is a genuine cost that has to clear a high bar.

Can you be your own BRP?

There is no licence and no prequalification — it is a contract, not a permit. But for ten trucks (order of magnitude 6 MWh/day) it is economically absurd: the collateral formula, a 24/7 schedule desk and EDI@Energy capability do not scale down. The realistic structures are:

  1. Supplier with a dynamic tariff. Zero effort, zero exposure, almost none of the value.
  2. Service BRP + your own Unterbilanzkreis. You keep the trading decisions and the upside; they keep the TSO relationship and the collateral. This is the structure the simulator models.
  3. Aggregator as BSP. Your depot joins someone else’s prequalified pool — with your supplier’s and BRP’s written consent.
  4. Own BSP prequalification. Only at pool scale. 10 × 350 kW is nominally 3.5 MW; reliably available is a much smaller, time-varying number.

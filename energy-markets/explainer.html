How the German electricity market
actually works

There is a market operator that clears day-ahead and intraday. There is a TSO that does balancing. There is a company offering to “be your BRP”. Those three sentences are all true and none of them tells you how the pieces fit together.

This page starts from the one physical fact that forces all of it to exist, explains what ancillary services are, what a balance group is, what happens inside an auction you cannot see, and what every line on your electricity bill actually pays for — then traces a single megawatt-hour from the forward market to the monthly invoice. Every counterparty named, every claim tied to a clause or a formula. No prior knowledge assumed.

Section 1 · start here

What the grid actually needs, and who sells it

Before any of the market machinery makes sense, it helps to know what problem it is all solving. There is only one, and it is brutally simple.

At every instant, the amount of electricity going into the grid must equal the amount coming out. Not on average over the day. Not roughly. At every instant.

Electricity is not stored in the wires. If consumption exceeds generation, the shortfall is taken out of the rotating mass of every generator on the system, they slow down, and the grid frequency falls below 50 Hz. If generation exceeds consumption, frequency rises. Drift far enough — roughly ±0.2 Hz before things get serious, ±2.5 Hz before equipment starts disconnecting itself — and the system begins to come apart.

Two very different jobs follow from that one sentence, and almost all the confusion in this area comes from mixing them up.

Job 1

Get the plan roughly right, in advance

Thousands of buyers and sellers agree in advance who will produce what and who will consume what, slot by slot. By the time delivery arrives, the plan already balances on paper.

This is the energy market — the day-ahead auction and the intraday market. It is a normal commodity market: you buy a thing, you pay for the thing, in €/MWh.

Job 2

Fix whatever the plan got wrong, in real time

No plan is ever right. Wind forecasts miss. A power station trips. A truck comes back from its shift 40 minutes late. Somebody has to hold equipment on standby, under contract, ready to move within seconds.

This is where ancillary services live. And it is a different kind of purchase: you are not buying energy, you are buying capability.

So what exactly is an “ancillary service”?

An ancillary service is anything the system operator needs in order to run the grid that is not the selling of energy itself. The EU definition (Directive 2019/944, Art. 2(48)) is deliberately broad: “a service necessary for the operation of a transmission or distribution system”.

an analogy

An airline sells you a seat. That is its product, and it is what appears on your ticket. But it cannot fly without air-traffic control, de-icing crews, a standby aircraft at the hub, ground power units and a fire service on the runway. Nobody buys a ticket for de-icing. It still has to exist, someone still has to be under contract to provide it, and someone still has to pay for it.

Energy is the seat. Ancillary services are everything else that makes the flight possible.

They are not variations on a theme. They solve genuinely different physical problems, and — this is the part that matters — they are bought in genuinely different ways.

Now the actual question: is the balancing market the same as the ancillary services market?

This question gets asked constantly and the honest answer has three parts. The short version is at the bottom if you want to skip ahead.

a · Balancing is inside ancillary services, not beside them

They are not two categories to compare. Balancing is family 1 of the five above. Every balancing product (FCR, aFRR, mFRR) is an ancillary service. Most ancillary services are not balancing.

So the relationship is containment, not rivalry — the same relationship as “cardiology” to “medicine”. Asking how they differ is asking the wrong shape of question.

b · There is no such thing as “the ancillary services market”

This is the part that actually resolves the confusion. “Ancillary services” is a category, not a marketplace. Look down the fourth column of the table above: one family is auctioned, one is a compliance obligation, one is negotiated bilaterally, one is not bought at all, and one is a legal instruction you cannot refuse.

There is no single venue, no single price, no single contract, and no single set of participants. Balancing is the only family of the five that is organised as a competitive market in Germany.

put another way

Asking “how does the ancillary services market differ from the balancing market?” is like asking how the transport market differs from the taxi market. The taxi market is a real, definable thing with prices and drivers you can hail. “The transport market” is an umbrella containing taxis, buses, freight rail and bicycles — organised in completely different ways, with different regulators and different money. You cannot buy a ticket for “transport”.

c · Two things make balancing unusual within the family

This is the “is there anything additional in balancing?” part of your question. Yes — two things, and neither has any equivalent in the other four families.

  1. It is split into two separate markets for the same physical thing. You are paid once in €/MW for standing ready (balancing capacity), and again in €/MWh if you are actually called (balancing energy). Two auctions, two prices, two merit orders, one asset. Nothing else in ancillary services works that way — a black-start contract is a single retainer, and reactive power is simply a condition of being connected.
  2. It has a financial twin that applies to everybody. Balancing is the only family that produces an imbalance price — the reBAP — which is charged to or paid to every single market participant, whether or not they have ever sold an ancillary service in their life. Nobody is ever “exposed to the black-start price”. Everybody, always, is exposed to the imbalance price.

What this means if someone offers you “ancillary services revenue”

In Germany, for a truck depot, the only genuinely sellable ancillary services are the three balancing products — FCR, aFRR and mFRR. Voltage support and black start are not open to you. Inertia is not bought. Redispatch is something that happens to you. So when a vendor says “ancillary services revenue”, the correct follow-up question is: which product, which direction, and which of the three payments are you quoting? If they cannot answer that in one sentence, the number is not real.

The three sentences worth memorising

  1. Ancillary services = everything the TSO needs that is not the selling of energy. It is a category, not a marketplace.
  2. Balancing = the frequency slice of that category. It is the one slice that really is a competitive market — in fact two markets (capacity and energy), plus a settlement process (imbalance) that catches everyone.
  3. Most of the rest of the category is not a market at all — it is obligation, bilateral contract, or a legal instruction you cannot refuse.

Section 3

The three payments — and why there have to be three

The same megawatt-hour can be paid for three times, under three contracts, with two different counterparties. That sounds like a swindle until you see what each payment is actually for. Then it becomes obvious.

the intuition, in one picture

Think of a town that contracts a private fire brigade.

  • The retainer. The town pays a fixed monthly fee just to have the crew sitting in the station, trained, with the engine fuelled. They are paid this whether or not there is a fire. That is balancing capacity.
  • The callout. When there is actually a fire, the brigade is paid again — per hour on site, for the water and the crew. Only if they are called. That is balancing energy.
  • The insurance excess. Completely separately, if your building was the one that caught fire because you left a heater on, you pay toward the damage — regardless of whether you have any relationship with the fire brigade at all. That is imbalance settlement.

The first two go to the people with the equipment. The third is charged to whoever caused the problem. They are unrelated contracts that happen to concern the same event.

The technical role. Owns or controls equipment that can change setpoint on command. Must pass prequalification with the TSO. These two payments are rewards for being useful — you bid, you win, you deliver.

The financial role. Owns nothing. This is not a reward and not a penalty — it is a price. It reprices your deviation at what energy was actually worth at that moment, in whichever direction the deviation pointed.

Why it matters

A pitch quoting “€/MW of aFRR” and a business case quoting “imbalance savings” are describing different contracts with different counterparties. Adding them together without checking is how flexibility business cases get built twice on the same megawatt.

A worked example: one Tuesday at Depot GmbH

Ten electric trucks, a 1.6 MW grid connection. The depot sits inside a service BRP’s balance group and has joined an aggregator’s prequalified aFRR pool. Here is every euro that moves, and who it moves between. Watch how the three payments never touch each other.

Payment 1 · capacity+ €48.00

At 09:00 on Monday the aggregator bids 1 MW of negative aFRR — a promise that the depot can charge harder than planned on command — for the 00:00–04:00 block on Tuesday. The bid clears. The depot has now sold an option.

1 MW × 4 hours × 12 €/MW·h = €48.00

Paid by the TSO to the BSP (the aggregator), who passes on its share. Paid whether or not the depot is ever called. If Tuesday night is uneventful, this €48 is the entire revenue — and the depot did nothing but keep chargers free.

Payment 2 · energy+ €5.10 net

At 01:15 Tuesday the grid is over-supplied — too much wind. PICASSO calls the pool for negative aFRR. The depot’s chargers ramp up for half an hour, absorbing 0.3 MWh more than planned.

0.3 MWh absorbed at the negative-direction price of 45 €/MWh → depot pays €13.50 otherwise bought on intraday at 62 €/MWh → would have cost €18.60 net benefit = 18.60 − 13.50 = €5.10

This is the bit people get backwards. In the negative direction you are being paid to consume, so the cash flow looks like buying energy cheaply rather than like receiving a cheque. The energy is also neutralised in the balance group, so it does not show up as an imbalance — you were told to do it.

Payment 3 · imbalance− €8.80

Entirely separately: at 16:45 a truck comes back from its shift 40 minutes late with a lower state of charge than forecast. To make its 06:00 departure the next morning it has to start charging immediately. The depot draws 0.08 MWh more in that quarter hour than its schedule said.

balance group is short by 0.08 MWh reBAP that quarter hour = 110 €/MWh positive price + short group → you pay 0.08 MWh × 110 €/MWh = €8.80

Nobody called you. Nobody bought anything from you. The TSO simply observed that your group promised one thing and did another, and priced the difference at what energy was worth at that instant. This payment would have happened even if the depot had never heard of the balancing market.

Three lines, three contracts, one Tuesday. Payments 1 and 2 arrive through the aggregator acting as BSP. Payment 3 arrives as a line on the BRP’s monthly invoice. They are settled by different processes, on different timescales, under different agreements — and a depot can be exposed to payment 3 while having no relationship at all with payments 1 and 2.

Payment 3 is the one that behaves strangely

Payments 1 and 2 are intuitive: do a thing, get paid. Payment 3 is the one that catches people out, because it can go either way. Whether you pay or get paid depends on two signs — the sign of the price, and the sign of your own deviation.

reBAPYour balance groupResult
positiveshort — drew more than promisedyou pay
positivelong — drew less than promisedyou are paid
negativeshortyou are paid
negativelongyou pay

Why the sign flip is the whole point

A positive reBAP means the system was short and energy was scarce at that instant. If you took extra, you made it worse — you pay. If you took less than promised, you helped — you are paid.

A negative reBAP means the system was over-supplied and the TSO was paying people to consume. Now the logic inverts: taking extra helped, so you are paid; taking less made it worse, so you pay.

The price is not measuring how badly you forecast. It is measuring what your error did to the system. Get that one idea and section 10 — the one-price versus two-price argument — writes itself.

Section 4

The timeline: what happens when, and who is in charge at each moment

One delivery day, from a week out to the invoice. The thing to hold onto is that the markets and the balancing do not happen one after the other — they overlap.

the whole timeline as one analogy

You book a restaurant table.

  • Day-ahead auction — you reserve a table for four at 8 pm, at tonight’s advertised price.
  • Nomination — you confirm the booking with the restaurant. Now they have staffed and bought food for four covers. This is the step that makes it real.
  • Intraday — at 6 pm you call: make it five. They can still adjust, but the kitchen has already shopped, so the fifth cover costs a bit more than the first four did.
  • Delivery — you turn up with six people.
  • Imbalance settlement — the restaurant charges you for the two extra covers at whatever the walk-in rate was that night. And here is the twist: if they were over-booked and turning people away, the extra covers were a problem and you pay a premium. If the place was half empty, your two extras helped — and in the electricity version of this story you would actually be paid for them.

The restaurant never takes over your booking. It fixes the evening, then bills you for the difference between what you promised and what you did. That is exactly the relationship between you and the TSO.

Look at the two green bars on the right. The TSO is activating reserves all day long — and at the same time, the blue bar shows you are still trading. They run in parallel. There is no moment where one hands over to the other.

No energy moves at this stage — it is pure arithmetic about something that already happened.

Three things people say that are not quite right

“You can’t trade after H-1.”
Inside Germany you can trade until the quarter hour minus 5 minutes. Liquidity thins and the spread widens, but the market is open.
“The TSO starts balancing at H-1.”
It bought the capacity at 08:00–10:00 the previous morning, and FCR has been acting every second since midnight. Balancing never starts, because it never stops.
“The TSO takes over your position.”
It never assumes your obligation. It is the balancer of last resort, not the counterparty of last resort. It fixes the system, then bills your BRP for your share of the error.

Why the TSO’s job is smaller than it sounds

At any moment, hundreds of balance groups are out of position — some short, some long. Before the TSO does anything, those errors cancel against each other. A supplier whose customers used less than forecast offsets a wind farm that produced less than forecast.

The TSO only ever has to cover the net residual. This is why the total volume settled as imbalance energy is routinely far larger than the balancing energy actually activated — and why “how much balancing energy did Germany use” and “how much imbalance was there” are two very different questions with two very different answers.

Section 6

Aggregation, explained properly

“Contractual aggregation” is a phrase that appears in every European flexibility document and is almost never explained. It is actually a simple idea about whose permission you need.

First: what is an aggregator trying to do?

Your depot on its own is too small and too unreliable to sell flexibility to a TSO. Ten trucks might be able to shift 1.4 MW — but only when they happen to be plugged in, which is not all day. An aggregator signs up dozens or hundreds of such sites, pools them, and sells the combined flexibility as one product. Individually you are noise; together you are a power plant.

To do that, the aggregator needs to be able to change what your chargers do — remotely, within seconds, without asking you each time. And that is where the problem starts.

The problem: your electricity is already somebody else’s business.

You buy power from a supplier. That supplier sits in a BRP’s balance group, and that BRP has already promised the TSO exactly how much your meter will draw in every quarter hour of tomorrow. If a third party suddenly makes your trucks charge harder at 01:15, three things happen that have nothing to do with you:

  1. Your meter records energy your supplier never planned to sell you — and has to go and buy.
  2. Your BRP’s balance group is now short, so it pays the imbalance price on the difference.
  3. Neither of them agreed to any of it, and neither of them is paid for it.
the analogy

You drive a company car with a fuel card paid by your employer. A delivery firm offers you money to run extra errands in the evening. Great for you — but the fuel comes off your employer’s card, and your employer neither agreed to it nor gets paid for it.

  • Contractual aggregation — the delivery firm must get your employer’s written agreement first, and the two of them settle the fuel between themselves. If your employer says no, the deal is dead.
  • Independent aggregation — the law says the delivery firm may hire you regardless, and a central clearing process automatically bills the delivery firm for the fuel and credits your employer. Your employer cannot block it.

Same activity, same money, completely different balance of power. That is the entire distinction.

Contractual aggregation — how Germany works today

Germany requires everyone affected to agree in advance and in writing. This is not a vague norm — it is a hard requirement of the prequalification process. Before a unit can be admitted to the balancing market, the applicant must file a certificate of knowledge and consent (Kenntnis- und Einverständniserklärung) from every market role involved that is not the same legal person:

Whose signatureWhy they get a vetoWhat they typically want
The connecting grid operator (DSO or TSO)The flexibility changes flows on their network, which they have to be able to operate safelyTechnical data; assurance it will not create a local constraint

The practical consequence is the important bit. Your existing supplier can simply refuse to sign — and refusing costs them nothing. This is exactly why most German flexibility offers arrive as a bundle: the aggregator is also the supplier and the BRP, so all three signatures are the same company’s. If you want an aggregator that is not your supplier, the first question to ask is not “what is the revenue share?” but “has my supplier agreed to sign?”

What actually happens to the energy — a worked example

Say your aggregator is called for 0.3 MWh of negative aFRR at 01:15 — meaning your chargers absorb 0.3 MWh more than anyone had planned. Follow it through the books.

If nobody had agreed anything

Your meter reads 0.3 MWh higher. Your BRP’s balance group is short by 0.3 MWh. The reBAP that quarter hour is 110 €/MWh, so the BRP pays €33 it never budgeted for. Meanwhile the aggregator collects the balancing-energy payment. Somebody else’s cost, the aggregator’s revenue. Unworkable — which is precisely why the rules do not allow it.

What actually happens

Because the BRP consented at prequalification, the TSO knows this 0.3 MWh was its own instruction. The volume is neutralised in the balance group: for settlement purposes the schedule is treated as if it had always included those 0.3 MWh.

balance group position = metered − delivered balancing energy 0.3 MWh drawn − 0.3 MWh instructed = 0 MWh imbalance

The BRP pays nothing. The aggregator is paid the balancing-energy price. The energy itself is settled between the aggregator and the TSO at the platform marginal price, not at your supplier’s tariff. Everyone ends up where they agreed to be.

This is why the consent matters mechanically, not just legally. The neutralisation only works because the TSO can link your metering point to a prequalified unit inside a pool whose BRP has signed. No signature, no link; no link, no neutralisation; no neutralisation, and the whole thing collapses into someone else’s imbalance bill.

Independent aggregation — the model Europe is moving toward

Article 17 of Directive (EU) 2019/944 requires Member States to let an aggregator act without the supplier’s or BRP’s agreement. The point is competition: if your supplier can veto every rival aggregator, there is no flexibility market, only flexibility bundled into supply contracts.

To make that work without robbing the supplier, two mechanisms replace the signature:

1 · Automatic imbalance adjustment

The delivered flexibility volume is removed from the host BRP’s position automatically, by the settlement system, without either party having to do anything. Same effect as the neutralisation above — but granted by regulation rather than by contract.

2 · Compensation between aggregator and supplier

The supplier bought energy it then did not sell (or had to supply energy it had not bought). A centrally calculated payment moves between the aggregator and the supplier/BRP to settle that, on a published formula rather than a negotiated one.

Section 7

The BRP and the balance group, from the beginning

This is the concept everything else hangs off, and it is almost never explained from scratch. So: from scratch.

What is a balance group?

the analogy

A Bilanzkreis (balance group) is a bank account for electricity.

Everything that flows in is a deposit: power stations feeding in, energy bought from other accounts. Everything that flows out is a withdrawal: customers consuming, energy sold to other accounts. And the rule is simply that the account must balance to exactly zero in every 15-minute period — not at the end of the day, not on average. Every period, on its own.

The BRP (Bilanzkreisverantwortlicher) is the account holder. If the account does not balance, the bank — the TSO — squares it for them, at a price the TSO sets after the fact. That price is the reBAP, and that is the whole of section 9.

Two facts make this unavoidable rather than optional:

Nothing is unassigned

Under § 4 StromNZV, every injection point and every withdrawal point in Germany must belong to a balance group at all times. Your depot’s meter is in somebody’s Bilanzkreis right now, whether you know it or not — almost certainly your supplier’s. There is no such thing as unassigned electricity.

It is a promise, not a forecast

By 14:30 the day before, the BRP has told the TSO exactly what the account will do in each of tomorrow’s 96 quarter hours. The TSO then sizes its reserves on the assumption that everybody keeps their promise. That is why breaking it has a price.

Section 8

Buying the power: what actually happens in the auction

The most common sticking point, asked almost word for word every time: “we don’t know the prices before we bid — so what exactly are we doing?” It is a very good question, and the answer changes how you think about the whole day.

You do not submit a price. You submit how much you want at every possible price.

A day-ahead bid is not “1.6 MW at 70 €/MWh”. It is a curve: “for the quarter hour 02:00–02:15, I will take 1.6 MW if the price is at or below 70; I will take 0.8 MW if it is between 70 and 120; above 120 I take nothing.” You are declaring your willingness to pay, not guessing the outcome.

How the auction resolves it

Every buyer submits a curve like that. Every seller submits the mirror image — “I will generate 400 MW if the price is at least 45”. The exchange collects all of them in secret (this is why it is called a blind auction — nobody sees anyone else’s bids, ever), stacks them into one demand curve and one supply curve, and finds where they cross.

Uniform pricing

Everyone who clears pays — or receives — the same price: the one where the curves cross. Not what they bid. This is why bidding a high limit is not dangerous: it only affects whether you get the energy, never what you pay for it.

It is blind

You never see other bids, before or after. You submit by 12:00, the EUPHEMIA algorithm clears all coupled European zones at once, and results appear around 12:45. There is no haggling and no second round.

One clearing per quarter hour

Since 1 October 2025 the German day-ahead auction clears 96 separate products, one per quarter hour. Each gets its own price. Your bid curve is per quarter hour too.

So what do you submit? Two honest options

Option 1 · price-dependent

“I’ll charge in this quarter hour only if it is cheap enough.”

02:00–02:15 → 1.6 MW at ≤ 70 €/MWh 02:00–02:15 → 0.0 MW above 70 €/MWh

This is what flexible demand is supposed to do, and it is the economically correct answer: your bid tells the market what your flexibility is actually worth.

The catch: if the price comes in above your limit you get nothing in that quarter hour. Do that across too many quarter hours and your trucks do not charge. You now need a fallback — which means, in practice, bidding a very high limit somewhere.

Option 2 · price-taker, against a forecast

“Forecast tomorrow’s prices, decide when to charge, then buy that shape at any price.”

forecast tomorrow’s 96 prices → pick the cheapest feasible charging windows → submit those volumes with a high price limit → they clear whatever the outcome

You are guaranteed the energy. You are not guaranteed to have picked the right hours — if your forecast was wrong, you have simply bought expensive power with certainty.

This is what almost everyone actually does, including every depot optimiser on the market. The skill is not in the bidding, it is in the forecast.

Be aware of how the simulator simplifies this. When you pick “cheapest hours” in the simulator, it optimises against the day’s actual cleared prices — that is, it assumes a perfect forecast. Real life is option 2 with an imperfect forecast, so the real-world gap between “cheapest hours” and “flat” is narrower than the simulator shows. Treat the simulator’s arbitrage saving as an upper bound, not an estimate.

And what is the BRP doing in all this?

This is the other half of the question, and the answer depends entirely on which contract you signed — which is why section 7 matters before this one. There are two arrangements:

They decide — “full supply”

The BRP forecasts your consumption from history, buys the energy it thinks you will need, nominates it, and sends you a bill at your agreed tariff. You are not involved in the auction at all and probably never see a day-ahead price.

Simple, safe — and there is nothing for flexible charging to capture, because your tariff does not change hour to hour.

You decide — they execute the simulator’s model

You send them a charging plan by their cut-off (often 13:00, earlier than the TSO’s 14:30). They place the order on the exchange in your name — you almost certainly are not an exchange member yourself — and then nominate the matching schedule to the TSO by 14:30.

The decision is yours; the access is theirs. That is precisely the service you are paying the €/MWh fee for.

One thing the BRP always does, in both arrangements: turn the trade into a nomination. A trade is a contract with a counterparty; a nomination is a promise to the system operator. Until 14:30 D-1 your purchase is just paperwork between traders — after it, the TSO has built its reserve plan around your quarter hours. That is the moment your plan becomes physically real, and it is the step that has no equivalent in any other commodity market.

Then intraday: a completely different mechanism

People assume intraday is “another auction”. Mostly it is not. Continuous intraday works like a share market: a live order book where buyers and sellers post offers and a trade happens the instant two of them overlap. There is no clearing price and no fixed moment — just whatever someone will deal at, right now.

What that means in practice

  • There is a bid–ask spread. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid, so every round trip costs you the gap — before any fee.
  • Liquidity thins as delivery approaches and near quiet quarter hours, so the spread widens exactly when you most need to trade.
  • You can trade until 5 minutes before the quarter hour inside Germany (60 minutes cross-border), but your schedule change to the TSO still needs a quarter hour of lead time.
  • There are also three intraday auctions (IDA1, IDA2, IDA3) that work like miniature day-ahead auctions — useful for liquidity, but the continuous book is where most adjustment happens.
Worked example− €5.63

At 11:00 you learn a truck will be back 40 minutes late, so you need 0.25 MWh less at 16:45 and 0.25 MWh more at 17:30 than you nominated.

sell 0.25 MWh at 16:45 · index 96.00 − spread 2.70 = 93.30 → + €23.33 buy 0.25 MWh at 17:30 · index 108.00 + spread 2.70 = 110.70 → − €27.68 fees 0.5 MWh × (0.12 + 0.035 + 0.50) €/MWh → − €0.33 ──────────────── net − €5.63

You paid €5.63 to move a quarter of a megawatt-hour by 45 minutes. Whether that was worth it depends entirely on what the reBAP turned out to be in those two quarter hours — which you will not know until weeks later. That uncertainty is the whole game.

The four sentences that answer the original question

  1. You never guess the price — you declare how much you want at each price, and the auction finds the price for you.
  2. Everyone who clears pays the same price, so a high bid limit buys certainty, not expense.
  3. In practice flexible buyers forecast tomorrow’s prices, choose their hours, and then bid to clear for certain — so the value comes from the forecast, not from the bidding.
  4. Your BRP is either deciding for you (full supply) or executing and nominating for you (the flexible case). Either way it is the BRP that turns the trade into a promise to the TSO.

Day-ahead 30 €/MWh, imbalance 33 when short and 27 when long. Read the last two columns as incentives. On the aggravating rows the two schemes are identical. They differ only on the helping rows — and there, two-price replaces a reward with the day-ahead price, i.e. with indifference.

Why single pricing won

  1. Direction, not magnitude. Two-price penalises deviation as such; one-price prices its effect on the system.
  2. Passive balancing is free reserve. Every MW BRPs supply by leaning the right way is a MW the TSO does not activate — and, through dimensioning, eventually a MW it does not procure.
  3. The imbalance price should equal the real-time value of energy. Two-price deliberately breaks that for half the market in every period.
  4. The revenue is a transfer. Under financial neutrality it is recycled anyway, so two-price functions as a tax on forecast error — falling hardest on variable renewables and small portfolios.
  5. It distorts intraday. Facing a spread against the imbalance price, a BRP trades at prices it knows to be bad purely to avoid it. Noise, not price discovery.
  6. It is the EU legal default. EB GL Art. 55 sets single pricing as the model; dual requires explicit approval.

The honest objection — and the real fix

Single pricing does create a speculative incentive to under- or over-nominate if you can forecast the system’s direction. The textbook remedy — cap the imbalance price back at the day-ahead price — is two-price, and throws out the cooperation incentive with the speculation incentive.

What real systems do instead:

  • Module 2. Forces a minimum spread against the intraday index, scaled by the system imbalance. Imbalance is then never cheaper than trading flat, in either direction, at all times.
  • Module 3. In tight periods the price escalates far beyond anything a speculator would risk.
  • Ziffer 5.2. Bilanzkreistreue is a contractual duty. Deliberate systematic imbalance is investigable under Ziffer 11.4, with REMIT on top.
  • 15-minute periods. They shrink both the structural imbalance and the window in which anyone forecasts the system better than the market.
The clean formulation. Single pricing with incentivising and scarcity components dominates dual pricing, because it preserves the property people want from dual pricing — imbalance is never cheaper than trading — without destroying the cooperation signal.

The Leistungspreis trap — and the lever nobody uses

A depot with fast chargers has an unusual load shape: very high power, very few hours. That combination is penalised twice by the German network tariff, and understanding why opens up the single largest saving available to you.

Why the tariff punishes you

German network charges for metered commercial customers (RLM) come in two variants, split at 2 500 Benutzungsstunden per year:

Benutzungsstunden = annual kWh ÷ annual peak kW

Above 2 500 hours you get a low per-kWh charge and a high per-kW charge. Below it, the opposite — an expensive per-kWh tariff.

Now run the depot’s numbers: roughly 1 000 MWh a year against a 1.6 MW peak.

1 000 000 kWh ÷ 1 600 kW ≈ 625 hours

Far below 2 500. You land in the expensive tariff — and you also pay the Leistungspreis on a peak you only touch for a few quarter hours a year.

The lever: § 19(2) StromNEV

German law lets you negotiate an individual network charge in two situations. One of them fits a depot almost perfectly:

§ 19(2) sentence 1 — atypische Netznutzung. If your annual peak reliably falls outside the high-load windows your network operator publishes (Hochlastzeitfenster), you are not contributing to the system peak the network was built for — so you can be charged individually. In practice the Leistungspreis is then billed on your highest peak within those windows rather than your highest peak overall, and the charge can fall to as little as 20 % of the standard rate.

§ 19(2) sentence 2 — intensive Netznutzung needs at least 7 000 Benutzungsstunden and 10 GWh a year. A truck depot will not reach that. Ignore it.

Two hard conditions. The agreement must be notified to the Bundesnetzagentur or your state regulator by 30 September of the year it is to apply. And it only works if you genuinely stay out of the windows — which means a charging controller that treats the HLZF as a hard constraint, every single day, not a preference.
A correction worth having. § 14a EnWG — the reduced grid charges for controllable consumption devices — gets quoted constantly in EV-charging business cases. It applies only to devices above 4.2 kW connected in low voltage and commissioned from 1 January 2024. A depot on a medium-voltage connection with RLM metering is outside its scope entirely. Your instruments are the Leistungspreis and § 19(2) StromNEV, not § 14a.

Section 12

Reading the simulator

Every screen, and what each number on it means. Open the simulator beside this section and work down.

What the simulator is for. It runs one delivery day for a ten-truck depot, quarter hour by quarter hour, and shows you where the money goes. It is a teaching instrument, not a forecasting tool — the prices are synthetic, and the point is to make the mechanisms visible, not to predict a number you can put in a business case.

Step 1 · Setup

1 · Pick the day

Four synthetic German days. They differ only in prices and system conditions — same fleet, same shifts, same forecast errors — so anything that changes between them is caused by the market, not by the depot.

Start with Typical weekday. Then run Windy spring day to see negative prices, and Scarcity event to see what one very bad quarter hour costs.

2 · Pick your BRP

The three archetypes from section 7. The row that matters most and looks most boring is “credits passed through”: a contract that charges you the full imbalance price but pays you only 60 % of it is a private two-price scheme, and the settlement screen will show you exactly what it costs.

These are three points on a six-dimensional space, not the only three contracts that exist — see section 7.

3 · The depot

Grid connection (kW) — the hard ceiling on total simultaneous charging. Raising it lets you charge faster in cheap hours, but it also raises the peak you will be billed for.

Battery per truck / charger power — how much energy each truck holds and how fast it can take it.

Required SoC at departure — the operational promise. The dispatcher will break your trading plan to keep this, which is exactly where imbalance comes from.

SoC floor — never discharge below this.

Battery degradation (€/kWh) — your assumed cost per kWh of throughput. The single most leveraged number in the whole model. Move it and watch every V2G conclusion change.

Round-trip efficiency — 94 % each way is about 88 % round trip, so a stored-and-returned kWh loses roughly an eighth of itself.

4 · Costs outside the energy price

This is the panel people skip, and it is where most of the money is. Section 11 has the full breakdown; in short:

Grid fees, levies, tax (€/MWh) — one combined figure standing in for the network Arbeitspreis, the KWKG / Offshore / § 19 levies (2.946 ct/kWh in 2026), Stromsteuer (2.050 ct/kWh) and the Konzessionsabgabe. The default of 95 €/MWh is a realistic German commercial figure. Note that this is often larger than the wholesale energy price itself.

Peak power charge (€/kW·a) — the Leistungspreis, billed on your single highest 15-minute peak of the year. The simulator pro-rates it to one day so you can see it alongside everything else; in reality one bad quarter hour sets it for twelve months.

Exchange fee, day-ahead / intraday — what EPEX charges per MWh traded. Intraday costs more because continuous trading is more expensive to run.

Clearing fee — what the clearing house charges to stand behind both sides of the trade.

Intraday half-spread — not a fee. It is the gap between the best buy and best sell price in the order book: you buy above the mid-price and sell below it, so a round trip costs you twice this number. It is the real price of changing your mind.

Spread widening near gate closure — a multiplier on that spread for quarter hours close to the moment you find out you have a problem. Liquidity thins exactly when you need it.

Cost of a missed departure — what it costs when a truck cannot leave. Deliberately large, because it should dominate every other consideration.

Step 2 · Day-ahead

You choose when to charge. That choice becomes your purchase and then your nomination to the TSO. Section 8 explains what is really happening behind the scenes.

StrategyWhat it doesWhat it teaches
Cheapest hoursFills the cheapest quarter hours in each truck’s plugged-in windowThe upper bound on arbitrage value — it assumes a perfect price forecast (see section 8)

Run “cheapest” and “flat” back to back and compare the whole settlement, not just the energy line. Cheapest charging concentrates load and pushes your peak to the grid limit; flat charging pays more per MWh but can cost less in total. That trade-off is the single most useful thing the simulator has to show you.

Step 3 · Intraday

At 11:00 on the delivery day, two forecast errors are revealed — a truck delayed, another back early. A third stays hidden until delivery, because that is the part no amount of trading can fix.

The one slider — how much of the revealed gap you close — is the whole decision. Closing it costs you the spread and the fees now, with certainty. Leaving it open costs you the imbalance price later, which you do not yet know. Slide it from 0 % to 100 % and watch the settlement screen. On a calm day, leaving the gap open is often cheaper. On a scarcity day it is not.

Step 4 · Delivery

What to look at

Nominated versus metered. Where the orange line leaves the blue area, operations overrode your trading plan — a truck had to charge to make its departure. That gap is the imbalance, and it is caused by the depot, not by the market.

The deviation bars. Blue where your deviation earned money, red where it cost money. Notice that the colour does not follow the direction of the deviation — it follows the sign flip from section 3.

Fleet state of charge. Should never touch the red floor line, and must be above the green departure line whenever a truck leaves.

The reBAP inspector

Drag the quarter-hour slider and watch which of the three modules binds. This is section 9 made concrete:

  • Module 1 binds most of the time — the price of the balancing energy actually activated.
  • Module 2 binds when activation was cheap but the system was still out of balance — it forces the price away from the intraday index so that being imbalanced is never cheaper than trading.
  • Module 3 binds only in genuine scarcity. Load the Scarcity event day to see it.

Step 5 · Settlement

The day’s invoice. Two things are worth more attention than the headline number:

The line-by-line table

Compare the subtotals, not the lines. On a typical run, network charges and levies exceed the entire energy bill, and the imbalance line — the thing everyone worries about — is among the smallest numbers on the page. That proportion is the lesson.

The three-contract comparison

The same day, the same fleet, the same trading — priced under all three BRP archetypes. Which one wins depends entirely on how volatile the reBAP was that day. Change the scenario and the winner changes. There is no best contract, only a best contract for a given risk appetite.

Five experiments worth running

  1. Cheapest versus flat, on the same day. Watch the energy line fall and the peak-power line rise. Which wins?
  2. Set battery degradation to 0.00 and then to 0.10 €/kWh with V2G on. The entire conclusion about V2G flips. That is the honest state of the art.
  3. Set the intraday close slider to 0 % on the windy day and then on the scarcity day. Same decision, opposite outcome.
  4. Lower the grid connection to 800 kW. Suddenly charging cannot be concentrated at all, the arbitrage disappears — and the peak-power charge halves.
  5. Switch BRP to pass-through on the scarcity day. This is what carrying your own tail risk actually feels like.
